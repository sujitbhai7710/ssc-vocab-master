Top 100 Rules For Error Spotting
💡 Subject-Verb Agreement (Rules 1 - 15)
Rule 1: The Core Rule
• Concept: A singular subject takes a singular verb (with '-s'). A plural subject takes a
plural verb (without '-s').
• Incorrect: The students of this batch learns very quickly.
• Correct: The students of this batch learn very quickly.
Rule 2: The "Of" Trap (Prepositional Phrases)
• Concept: Do not look at the noun right before the verb. Look for the real subject
before the word "of".
• Incorrect: The quality of these mangoes are not good.
• Correct: The quality of these mangoes is not good. (Subject is 'quality', not
'mangoes')
Rule 3: The "With" Family
• Concept: When phrases like as well as, along with, together with, with, accompanied
by, in addition to connect two subjects, the verb agrees only with the first subject.
• Incorrect: The teacher, along with her students, were present.
• Correct: The teacher, along with her students, was present.
Rule 4: Either / Or and Neither / Nor (Proximity Rule)
• Concept: When subjects are joined by either... or or neither... nor, the verb agrees
with the nearest subject (the one closest to the verb).
• Incorrect: Neither the manager nor the employees was present.
• Correct: Neither the manager nor the employees were present.
Rule 5: Each and Every
• Concept: Words like Each, Every, Everyone, Someone, Nobody, Anyone are always
singular and take a singular verb.
• Incorrect: Each of the boys have a laptop.
• Correct: Each of the boys has a laptop.
Rule 6: The "One of the" Structure
• Concept: The formula is: One of + Plural Noun + Singular Verb.
• Incorrect: One of my friends are a doctor.
• Correct: One of my friends is a doctor.

Rule 7: One of the + Relative Pronoun (Who/Which/That)
• Concept: If "who", "which", or "that" comes after the plural noun in the "one of"
structure, the verb becomes plural because it refers to the whole group.
• Incorrect: He is one of those men who tells lies.
• Correct: He is one of those men who tell lies.
Rule 8: A Number of vs. The Number of
• Concept:
o A number of + Plural Noun = Plural Verb (means 'many').
o The number of + Plural Noun = Singular Verb (means 'a specific total count').
• Incorrect: A number of students has failed.
• Correct: A number of students have failed.
• Incorrect: The number of accident cases have increased.
• Correct: The number of accident cases has increased.
Rule 9: Collective Nouns (Jury, Team, Committee)
• Concept: If the group works together as one body, use a singular verb. If the
members are fighting or acting individually, use a plural verb.
• Correct: The jury is giving its verdict. (United)
• Correct: The jury were divided in their opinions. (Divided)
Rule 10: Sums of Money, Distance, and Time
• Concept: When a specific amount of money, distance, or time is thought of as a
single lump sum, it takes a singular verb.
• Incorrect: Ten miles are a long distance to walk.
• Correct: Ten miles is a long distance to walk.
Rule 11: Many a / More than one
• Concept: The phrase "Many a" or "More than one" is always followed by a
singular noun and a singular verb.
• Incorrect: Many a students have failed the exam.
• Correct: Many a student has failed the exam.
Rule 12: Nouns that look plural but are singular
• Concept: Names of subjects (Physics, Mathematics), games (Billiards, Darts), and
diseases (Measles, Rickets) take a singular verb.
• Incorrect: Mathematics are a difficult subject.
• Correct: Mathematics is a difficult subject.
Rule 13: "Nothing but"
• Concept: When a sentence starts with "Nothing but", the verb must always be
singular, whether the noun is singular or plural.
• Incorrect: Nothing but trees were seen in the valley.

• Correct: Nothing but trees was seen in the valley.
Rule 14: Sentences starting with "There" or "Here"
• Concept: In these sentences, the real subject comes after the verb. Check that noun to
decide if the verb should be singular or plural.
• Incorrect: There is many problems in this city.
• Correct: There are many problems in this city.
Rule 15: Two nouns making one unified dish/concept
• Concept: When two nouns combine to form one single idea or dish (Bread and butter,
Rice and curry, Slow and steady), they take a singular verb.
• Incorrect: Bread and butter are my favorite breakfast.
• Correct: Bread and butter is my favorite breakfast.
📝 Noun Traps (Rules 16 - 30)
Rule 16: Uncountable Nouns (Never use 's' or 'es')
• Concept: Nouns like Scenery, Furniture, Advice, Information, Luggage, Baggage,
Poetry, Hair are uncountable. You cannot make them plural by adding 's'. They
always take a singular verb.
• Incorrect: The furnitures of my house are new.
• Correct: The furniture of my house is new.
Rule 17: How to count uncountable nouns
• Concept: To count words like advice or information, use phrases like "a piece of" or
"pieces of".
• Incorrect: He gave me two advices.
• Correct: He gave me two pieces of advice.
Rule 18: Nouns that are always Plural in form
• Concept: Tools or clothing items with two symmetric parts (Scissors, Trousers,
Pants, Spectacles, Binoculars, Tongs) are always plural and take a plural verb.
• Incorrect: Where is my scissors?
• Correct: Where are my scissors?
Rule 19: "A pair of" changes the rule
• Concept: If you add the phrase "A pair of" before scissors, spectacles, or trousers,
the verb changes to singular.
• Incorrect: A pair of shoes were bought by him.
• Correct: A pair of shoes was bought by him.

Rule 20: Nouns that look singular but are Plural
• Concept: Words like Cattle, Clergy, Gentry, Poultry, Peasantry, Folk, People, Police
look singular because they don't end in 's', but they are always plural and take a
plural verb.
• Incorrect: The cattle is grazing in the field.
• Correct: The cattle are grazing in the field.
Rule 21: Hyphenated Nouns cannot be plural
• Concept: A noun used as a modifier with a hyphen must never be in plural form.
• Incorrect: He gave me a five-rupees note.
• Correct: He gave me a five-rupee note.
Rule 22: Numbers + Noun of Measurement
• Concept: When a definite number comes before words like Pair, Score, Dozen,
Hundred, Thousand, Million, do not add 's' to them.
• Incorrect: I bought two dozens bananas.
• Correct: I bought two dozen bananas.
Rule 23: Indefinite plural of measurement words
• Concept: If words like hundred or dozen do not have a number before them, they can
take an 's' along with the word "of".
• Correct: Hundreds of people attended the rally.
Rule 24: Noun + Preposition + Same Noun
• Concept: When the exact same noun is repeated after a preposition, both nouns must
be singular.
• Incorrect: Rows upon rows of marble houses look beautiful.
• Correct: Row upon row of marble houses looks beautiful.
Rule 25: Words that don't change in Plural
• Concept: Words like Deer, Sheep, Fish, Offspring have the exact same form in both
singular and plural. Never write "sheeps".
• Incorrect: I saw many sheeps in the meadow.
• Correct: I saw many sheep in the meadow.
Rule 26: Possession with Non-living things
• Concept: Do not use apostrophe-s ('s) with non-living objects. Use the "of" structure
instead.
• Incorrect: The table's leg was broken.
• Correct: The leg of the table was broken.

Rule 27: Joint vs. Separate Possession
• Concept: If two people own the same thing together, add 's to the last name only. If
they own different things separately, add 's to both names.
• Correct: Sham and Ram's joint account. (One account)
• Correct: Sham's and Ram's cars. (Two different cars)
Rule 28: Nouns ending in 's' sound (Hissing sound)
• Concept: For singular nouns ending in a hissing 's' sound (Keats, Dickens, Jesus), just
add an apostrophe (') at the end for possession, not another 's'.
• Incorrect: Keats's poems are romantic.
• Correct: Keats' poems are romantic.
Rule 29: Compound Noun Plurals
• Concept: To pluralize a compound noun, add 's' to the root/principal word, not the
auxiliary words.
• Incorrect: My brother-in-laws have arrived.
• Correct: My brothers-in-law have arrived.
Rule 30: "As regards" vs. "As regard"
• Concept: The correct idiomatic phrase is always "as regards" or "in regard to". Never
mix them up.
• Incorrect: In regards to your letter...
• Correct: In regard to your letter...
👥 Pronoun Adjustments (Rules 31 - 45)
Rule 31: Order of Personal Pronouns (Normal Situations)
• Concept: When talking about general or good things, the order of pronouns must be
2nd Person (You) ➔ 3rd Person (He/She/They) ➔ 1st Person (I). (Trick: 231)
• Incorrect: I, you and he will study together.
• Correct: You, he and I will study together.
Rule 32: Order of Personal Pronouns (Mistakes/Blame)
• Concept: When confessing a mistake, crime, or guilt, the order switches to 1st
Person (I) ➔ 2nd Person (You) ➔ 3rd Person (He/They). (Trick: 123)
• Incorrect: You, he and I committed the crime.
• Correct: I, you and he committed the crime.
Rule 33: Comparison of Pronouns
• Concept: Always compare the same cases. Subject pronouns are compared with
subject pronouns (after words like "than" or "as").
• Incorrect: He is faster than me.

• Correct: He is faster than I (am).
Rule 34: Pronouns after "Let" and Prepositions
• Concept: Always use an object pronoun (me, him, her, us, them) after the word
"Let" or after any preposition.
• Incorrect: Let you and I solve this problem.
• Correct: Let you and me solve this problem.
Rule 35: Each other vs. One another
• Concept: Use "Each other" when talking about two people. Use "One another" for
more than two people.
• Incorrect: The two brothers fought with one another.
• Correct: The two brothers fought with each other.
Rule 36: Who vs. Whom
• Concept:
o Use Who when it acts as the subject performing the action (Replaceable with
He/They).
o Use Whom when it acts as the object receiving the action (Replaceable with
Him/Them).
• Correct: Who wrote this letter? (He wrote it)
• Correct: Whom did you invite? (You invited him)
Rule 37: Relative Pronoun Match
• Concept: The verb after who/which/that must match the noun directly before it (the
antecedent).
• Incorrect: It is I who is responsible.
• Correct: It is I who am responsible. (Matches 'I')
Rule 38: Use of "That" instead of "Who" or "Which"
• Concept: Always use "That" after words like: All, Same, Only, Any, None, Little,
Nothing and after Superlative adjectives.
• Incorrect: This is the best book which I have ever read.
• Correct: This is the best book that I have ever read.
Rule 39: "Everybody / Everyone" Gender Tracking
• Concept: When words like Everyone, Everybody, Each are used, track them using
masculine singular pronouns (his/him) by default unless the gender is clearly female.
Do not use "their".
• Incorrect: Everyone should do their duty.
• Correct: Everyone should do his duty.

Rule 40: "One" must follow "One's"
• Concept: If the subject of your sentence is the indefinite pronoun "One", you must
use "one's" for possession throughout the sentence, not "his" or "their".
• Incorrect: One must do his duty honestly.
• Correct: One must do one's duty honestly.
Rule 41: Avoid Reflexive Pronouns as Subjects
• Concept: Words ending in -self (Myself, Himself, Yourself) cannot act as the subject
of a sentence without a preceding noun or pronoun.
• Incorrect: Myself am Dr. Roy.
• Correct: I am Dr. Roy. / I myself am Dr. Roy.
Rule 42: Prohibited Reflexive Pronouns
• Concept: Do not use reflexive pronouns (myself, yourself) after verbs like: Keep,
Break, Stop, Turn, Qualify, Bathe, Move, Hide.
• Incorrect: He hid himself behind the heavy curtain.
• Correct: He hid behind the heavy curtain.
Rule 43: Mandatory Reflexive Pronouns
• Concept: You must use a reflexive pronoun object after these verbs if no direct object
is present: Avail, Adapt, Absent, Amuse, Resign, Reconcile, Enjoy, Exert.
• Incorrect: He absented from the class yesterday.
• Correct: He absented himself from the class yesterday.
Rule 44: "It is..." structure case
• Concept: When a pronoun follows constructions like "It is" or "It was", it must be in
the subject case.
• Incorrect: It was him who helped the poor old man.
• Correct: It was he who helped the poor old man.
Rule 45: Both can never be Negative
• Concept: Do not use "not" with the word "Both". Instead, use "Neither".
• Incorrect: Both of them did not go to school.
• Correct: Neither of them went to school.
⚡ Adjective & Adverb Modifiers (Rules 46 - 60)
Rule 46: Double Comparatives are Prohibited
• Concept: Never use two comparative indicators (more and -er) together in the same
modifier phrase.
• Incorrect: He is more wiser than his younger brother.
• Correct: He is wiser than his younger brother.

Rule 47: Adjectives ending in "-ior"
• Concept: Adjectives like Senior, Junior, Superior, Inferior, Prior, Preferable are
followed by the preposition "to", never "than".
• Incorrect: Milk is preferable than tea.
• Correct: Milk is preferable to tea.
Rule 48: Any other in Comparative Degree
• Concept: When comparing an item to the rest of its own group using comparative
degree, use the phrase "any other" to exclude it from the rest of the group.
• Incorrect: Iron is more useful than any metal.
• Correct: Iron is more useful than any other metal.
Rule 49: As... as / So... as
• Concept: Use "As... as" for positive sentences, and "So... as" or "As... as" for
negative sentences. The adjective inside must be in its simple base form (positive
degree).
• Incorrect: He is as wiser as his father.
• Correct: He is as wise as his father.
Rule 50: Too... to structure
• Concept: The combination "Too... to" carries a negative meaning by itself (meaning
"so much that it is impossible"). Do not add another negative word like "not".
• Incorrect: He is too weak not to walk.
• Correct: He is too weak to walk. (Means he is so weak that he cannot walk)
Rule 51: Position of "Enough"
• Concept: The word "Enough" is placed after the adjective it describes, but before
nouns.
• Incorrect: He is enough wise to understand your trick.
• Correct: He is wise enough to understand your trick.
Rule 52: Much vs. Very
• Concept:
o Use Very before simple base adjectives (Positive degree) and Present
Participles (verb+ing).
o Use Much before comparative adjectives (Comparative degree) and Past
Participles (verb-v3).
• Correct: It is a very interesting book.
• Correct: I am much interested in this structural project.
Rule 53: Little vs. Few (The Essential Scale)
• Concept:
o Few = Plural Countable Nouns (Books, Days).

===== PAGE 11 =====
o Little = Uncountable Nouns (Water, Milk, Knowledge).
• Incorrect: There are less people today.
• Correct: There are fewer people today.
Rule 54: Meaning differences: A few / A little
• Concept:
o Few/Little = Almost zero (Negative meaning).
o A few/A little = Some amount (Positive meaning).
o The few/The little = All that is remaining.
• Correct: A little knowledge is a dangerous thing.
Rule 55: Adverbs modify Verbs (Not Adjectives)
• Concept: To describe an action (verb), use an adverb (usually ending in '-ly'), not a
base adjective.
• Incorrect: She speaks beautiful.
• Correct: She speaks beautifully.
Rule 56: Verbs of Sensation (The Exception)
• Concept: Verbs of sensory perception (Taste, Smell, Feel, Sound, Look, Be, Become)
take an adjective, not an adverb.
• Incorrect: The food smells deliciously.
• Correct: The food smells delicious.
Rule 57: Hard vs. Hardly
• Concept:
o Hard = With great effort / solid.
o Hardly = Scarcely / Almost not at all (Negative word).
• Incorrect: He hardly worked and topped the exam.
• Correct: He worked hard and topped the exam.
Rule 58: Late vs. Lately
• Concept:
o Late = Not on time.
o Lately = Recently (Used mostly in Present Perfect tense).
• Correct: I have not seen him lately.
Rule 59: Inversion with Negative Adverbs
• Concept: When a sentence starts with a negative adverb like Hardly, Scarcely,
Seldom, Barely, No sooner, the helping verb must come before the subject.
• Incorrect: Seldom I have seen such a beautiful painting.
• Correct: Seldom have I seen such a beautiful painting.

Rule 60: Double Negatives are Prohibited
• Concept: Do not use negative words like not or never in the same clause with words
that already have a negative meaning, such as Hardly, Scarcely, Until, Unless, Forbid,
Prohibit, Lest.
• Incorrect: Unless you do not work hard, you will fail.
• Correct: Unless you work hard, you will fail.
⏳ Verbs & Tense Precision (Rules 61 - 75)
Rule 61: Since / For with Perfect Tenses
• Concept: When a time duration is given using Since (point in time) or For (period of
time), use a Perfect Tense or Perfect Continuous Tense, never a simple continuous
tense.
• Incorrect: I am living here since five years.
• Correct: I have been living here for five years.
Rule 62: Two actions in the Past (Past Perfect Rule)
• Concept: When two actions happened in the past, the older action takes the Past
Perfect Tense (Had + V3). The more recent action takes the Simple Past Tense (V2).
• Incorrect: The train departed before I reached the station.
• Correct: The train had departed before I reached the station.
Rule 63: Future Tense after Time/Condition Clauses
• Concept: Do not use "will" or "shall" in clauses starting with conditional or time
words like If, When, Before, After, Until, Unless, As soon as. Use Simple Present
instead.
• Incorrect: If it will rain, we will cancel the match.
• Correct: If it rains, we will cancel the match.
Rule 64: Conditional Clauses — Type 3 (Imaginary Past)
• Concept: If the conditional clause uses "Had + V3", the main result clause must use
"would have + V3".
• Incorrect: If you worked hard, you would have passed the exam.
• Correct: If you had worked hard, you would have passed the exam.
Rule 65: Unfulfilled Wish / Imaginary Conditions
• Concept: For imaginary or hypothetical sentences starting with I wish, If only, As
though, always use the plural past verb "Were", regardless of whether the subject is
singular or plural.
• Incorrect: I wish I was a king.
• Correct: I wish I were a king.

Rule 66: It is high time...
• Concept: Phrases like It is time, It is high time, It is about time must be followed by a
subject and a verb in the Simple Past Tense (V2).
• Incorrect: It is high time we start studying.
• Correct: It is high time we started studying.
Rule 67: "Lest" must follow "Should"
• Concept: The conjunction "Lest" means "for fear that". It is strictly paired with the
helping verb "Should". Do not add "not" to it.
• Incorrect: Run fast lest you will miss the train.
• Correct: Run fast lest you should miss the train.
Rule 68: Habitual Actions / Universal Truths
• Concept: General truths, scientific laws, and daily habits are always written in the
Simple Present Tense, even if the reporting introduction is in the past.
• Incorrect: Our teacher said that the earth revolved around the sun.
• Correct: Our teacher said that the earth revolves around the sun.
Rule 69: Stative Verbs (No "-ing" form)
• Concept: Verbs of mental state, emotion, or ownership (Love, Hate, Know,
Understand, Own, Belong, Possess) are generally not used in the continuous (-ing)
form.
• Incorrect: I am knowing the answer.
• Correct: I know the answer.
Rule 70: Gerund after "Look forward to"
• Concept: The prepositional phrases Look forward to, Used to, With a view to,
Addicted to, Devoted to must be followed by a Gerund (Verb+ing), not a base verb
infinitive.
• Incorrect: I am looking forward to meet you.
• Correct: I am looking forward to meeting you.
Rule 71: Split Infinitive Error
• Concept: Do not place an adverb between the word "to" and the base verb
(Infinitive). Put the adverb after the verb.
• Incorrect: You need to carefully check the documents.
• Correct: You need to check the documents carefully.
Rule 72: Bare Infinitive after "Make, Let, Bid"
• Concept: Verbs like Make, Let, Bid, Watch, Hear take a plain verb without "to" (Bare
Infinitive) when used in the active voice.
• Incorrect: He made me to cry.
• Correct: He made me cry.

Rule 73: Dangling Participles (Subject Misalignment)
• Concept: An introductory participle clause (-ing phrase) must clearly align with the
subject that follows it. If it doesn't, the sentence structure breaks down.
• Incorrect: Walking in the park, a snake bit him. (Was the snake walking in the park?)
• Correct: While he was walking in the park, a snake bit him.
Rule 74: As soon as... No balancing word
• Concept: Do not add "then" after using the introductory phrase "As soon as". A
simple comma is enough.
• Incorrect: As soon as the thief saw the police, then he ran away.
• Correct: As soon as the thief saw the police, he ran away.
Rule 75: Since as a Time Marker (Past vs Present Perfect)
• Concept: When "since" acts as a time connection link:
o Clause after since ➔ Simple Past (V2).
o Main clause before since ➔ Present Perfect (Has/Have + V3).
• Incorrect: Many things changed since he left the city.
• Correct: Many things have changed since he left the city.
🔗 Conjunction Connectors (Rules 76 - 85)
Rule 76: Scarcely / Hardly... When
• Concept: Always pair the introductory adverbs Hardly and Scarcely with "when" or
"before". Never use "than".
• Incorrect: Hardly had I arrived at the station than the train left.
• Correct: Hardly had I arrived at the station when the train left.
Rule 77: No Sooner... Than
• Concept: The introductory comparative phrase "No sooner" must be paired with
"than". Never use "then" or "when".
• Incorrect: No sooner did I open the door when the bird flew out.
• Correct: No sooner did I open the door than the bird flew out.
Rule 78: Not only... But also (Balance Rule)
• Concept: The phrases Not only and But also must be placed right before matching
parts of speech (Parallel placement).
• Incorrect: He not only lost his home but also his job.
• Correct: He lost not only his home but also his job. (Both sit right before nouns)
Rule 79: Although / Though... Yet
• Concept: Conjunctions like Although or Though are paired either with "yet" or a
simple comma. Never use "but".

• Incorrect: Although he worked hard, but he failed.
• Correct: Although he worked hard, yet he failed. / Although he worked hard, he
failed.
Rule 80: Between... And
• Concept: The preposition "Between" is strictly paired with the conjunction "and".
Never use "to".
• Incorrect: Contact us between 10 AM to 5 PM.
• Correct: Contact us between 10 AM and 5 PM.
Rule 81: From... To
• Concept: If you prefer to use the word "to" for a range, switch the introductory word
from "between" to "From".
• Correct: Contact us from 10 AM to 5 PM.
Rule 82: "So... that" vs. "Too... to"
• Concept: Do not mix up these two structures. It is either So + Adjective + That
clause or Too + Adjective + To infinitive.
• Incorrect: He is too weak that he cannot walk.
• Correct: He is so weak that he cannot walk.
Rule 83: Unless vs. Until
• Concept:
o Until is tied to a Time threshold.
o Unless is tied to a Condition.
• Correct: Wait here until I return. (Time)
• Correct: You will fail unless you study. (Condition)
Rule 84: Avoid "That" with Interrogative Sentences
• Concept: In indirect speech, do not use the conjunction "that" before question words
like Who, Which, What, Why, When, How, If, Whether.
• Incorrect: He asked me that why I was late.
• Correct: He asked me why I was late.
Rule 85: "Whether" is paired with "Or"
• Concept: When offering choices or showing doubt with options, use Whether... or.
Do not use "if".
• Incorrect: I do not know if he will come or not.
• Correct: I do not know whether he will come or not.

📍 Preposition Mastery (Rules 86 - 95)
Rule 86: No Preposition after Transitive Verbs
• Concept: Do not add prepositions after active verbs like Discuss, Enter, Marry,
Order, Resemble, Comprise.
• Incorrect: We discussed about the matter.
• Correct: We discussed the matter.
Rule 87: Enter vs. Enter into
• Concept:
o Do not use "into" when walking physically inside a room or building.
o You can use "into" when entering agreements, alliances, or business deals.
• Incorrect: He entered into the classroom.
• Correct: He entered the classroom.
Rule 88: Comprise vs. Consist of
• Concept: The verb "Comprise" means consist of. Do not use the word "of" right
after comprise in active voice sentences.
• Incorrect: The book comprises of ten chapters.
• Correct: The book comprises ten chapters. / The book consists of ten chapters.
Rule 89: Died of vs. Died from
• Concept:
o Use Died of for internal causes like diseases, hunger, or thirst (Died of
cancer).
o Use Died from for external causes like overwork, wounds, or poison (Died
from food poisoning).
• Correct: He died of cholera.
Rule 90: Angry with vs. Angry at
• Concept:
o Use Angry with a person.
o Use Angry at a thing, action, or behavior.
• Correct: I am angry with you, but I am angry at your behavior.
Rule 91: Blind in vs. Blind to
• Concept:
o Use Blind in when referring to physical loss of sight (Blind in the left eye).
o Use Blind to when someone ignores faults or mistakes (Blind to his son's
mistakes).
• Correct: A father should not be blind to his child's faults.

Rule 92: Senior/Junior take "to"
• Concept: As covered in Rule 47, Latin comparative adjectives never take "than".
• Incorrect: He is junior than me.
• Correct: He is junior to me.
Rule 93: In vs. Into (State of Motion)
• Concept: Use In to describe something that is already static inside a place. Use Into
to show movement from the outside to the inside.
• Correct: He is sitting in the room.
• Correct: He jumped into the swimming pool.
Rule 94: Beside vs. Besides
• Concept:
o Beside = By the side of / next to.
o Besides = In addition to / apart from.
• Incorrect: Sit besides me.
• Correct: Sit beside me.
Rule 95: Accompanied by vs. Accompanied with
• Concept: Use Accompanied by for individuals/people, and Accompanied with for
objects or things.
• Correct: The PM was accompanied by his bodyguards.
• Correct: Fever is often accompanied with a headache.
🛠️ Redundancy & Structure (Rules 96 - 100)
Rule 96: Return / Revert back
• Concept: The prefixes re- in words like Return, Revert, Retreat, Reimburse already
mean "back". Adding the word "back" after them is repetitive and incorrect.
• Incorrect: Please return back my book.
• Correct: Please return my book.
Rule 97: Reason why... because
• Concept: The phrase "The reason why" already explains cause. Do not use the word
"because" or "due to" later in the same statement. Use "that" instead.
• Incorrect: The reason why he failed was because he didn't study.
• Correct: The reason why he failed was that he didn't study.
Rule 98: Supposing if
• Concept: Both "Supposing" and "If" serve the exact same purpose to introduce a
condition. Never use them right next to each other.
• Incorrect: Supposing if it rains, what will we do?

Rule 99: Cousin brother / Cousin sister
• Concept: The word "Cousin" includes both genders. Adding "brother" or "sister"
after it is redundant in English grammar. Use context clues like he or she to show their
gender.
• Incorrect: She is my cousin sister.
• Correct: She is my cousin.
Rule 100: Final Conclusion / Twin Brothers
• Concept: A conclusion is always final, and twins always come in pairs. Adding words
like "final" or "twin" before them creates unnecessary repetition.
• Incorrect: This is the final conclusion of our research project.
• Correct: This is the conclusion of our research project.

• Answer: (C)

o Since $\rightarrow$ Used for a specific fixed point in time (since 2015, since

o For $\rightarrow$ Used for a duration or total period of time (for 5 years, for 3

Q27

• Answer: (C)
• Explanation: When two past actions happen sequentially, the earlier action must be
