# Manisha Bansal — 120 Rules of Grammar

Source: *120 Rules of Grammar* by Nimisha (Manisha) Bansal. 
120 rules with explanations, usage notes, examples, and memory tricks.

---

## Rule 1: Each vs. Every

**Category:** determiners


Both are used with singular verbs, but they differ in focus. Each focuses on individuals one by one, while Every treats all as a collective group.


**Usage**

- **Each** — Individual focus — can be a pronoun or adjective (used for 2 or more).
- **Every** — Group/collective focus — adjective only (used for 3 or more).


**Examples**

- ✓ Each student is ready.
- ✓ Every boy is present.
- ✗ Every of the students is here.

**Trick:** Each = One by one | Every = All together


## Rule 2: Both + Not → Neither…Nor

**Category:** conjunctions


When negating both subjects, never use Both…not. Always replace it with Neither…nor for correct and natural English.


**Usage**

- **Incorrect** — Both Ram and Shyam are not going.
- **Correct** — Neither Ram nor Shyam is going.


**Examples**

- ✓ Neither Ram nor Shyam is going.
- ✓ Neither the dog nor the cat is here.
- ✗ Both of them are not present.

**Trick:** Both + Not → Replace with Neither…nor always.


## Rule 3: Who / Whom / Which / That

**Category:** pronouns


These relative pronouns replace nouns in sentences. Choosing the right one depends on whether the noun is a person, a thing, or how it functions in the clause.


**Usage**

- **Who** — Subject — for people and pets.
- **Whom** — Object — for people only.
- **Which** — For things and wild animals.
- **That** — Universal — people, animals, things. Use after: all, any, only, same, none, superlatives.


**Examples**

- ✓ The boy who came is my friend.
- ✓ Whom did you call? (Him = Whom)
- ✓ This is the same pen that I lost.
- ✓ The cat which ran away is mine.

**Trick:** Who = He | Whom = Him | Which = Things | That = Universal connector


## Rule 4: Much vs. Many

**Category:** determiners


Much is used with uncountable nouns (things you can't count individually). Many is used with countable nouns (things with a clear plural form).


**Usage**

- **Much** — Uncountable nouns — water, sugar, time, money.
- **Many** — Countable nouns — pens, books, boys, students.


**Examples**

- ✓ How much water do you need?
- ✓ How many pens do you have?
- ✗ How many sugar is left?

**Trick:** If you can count it → Many | If you can't → Much


## Rule 5: Little / A Little / The Little

**Category:** determiners


All three are used with uncountable nouns, but they carry different meanings based on the context — negative, positive, or specific.


**Usage**

- **Little** — Almost none — negative idea (not enough).
- **A Little** — Some — positive idea (a small but sufficient amount).
- **The Little** — All of the small amount that exists — specific.


**Examples**

- ✓ Little milk was left, so I couldn't make tea.
- ✓ A little milk is still left — enough for one cup.
- ✓ The little milk that was left was spoiled.

**Trick:** Little = Negative | A little = Positive | The little = Specific


## Rule 6: Few / A Few / The Few

**Category:** determiners


All three are used with countable nouns. Just like Little/A Little/The Little, they indicate negative, positive, or specific quantity.


**Usage**

- **Few** — Almost none — negative idea.
- **A Few** — Some — positive idea.
- **The Few** — All of the limited group — specific.


**Examples**

- ✓ Few books were interesting in the library. (negative — hardly any)
- ✓ A few books caught my attention. (positive — some)
- ✓ The few books I had were enough to prepare.

**Trick:** Few = Negative | A few = Positive | The few = Specific group


## Rule 7: Some vs. Any

**Category:** determiners


Some is used in affirmative sentences, offers, and requests. Any is used in negative sentences and questions.


**Usage**

- **Some** — Positive statements, offers, polite requests.
- **Any** — Negative sentences and questions.


**Examples**

- ✓ I need some help. (positive)
- ✓ Can you give me some advice? (polite request)
- ✓ Do you have any sugar? (question)
- ✓ I don't have any time.

**Trick:** Some = Yes vibes | Any = Question / No vibes


## Rule 8: Since vs. For

**Category:** prepositions


Since refers to a specific point in time (when something started). For refers to a duration — how long something has continued.


**Usage**

- **Since** — A specific starting point — since 5 PM, since 2019, since childhood.
- **For** — A duration of time — for 2 hours, for 3 years, for a long time.


**Examples**

- ✓ I have been studying since 5 PM.
- ✓ I have been studying for 2 hours.
- ✗ I have been studying since 2 hours.

**Trick:** Since = Starting point (when) | For = How long (duration)


## Rule 9: Who vs. Whom

**Category:** pronouns


Who acts as the subject (doing the action). Whom acts as the object (receiving the action). Replace with He/Him to test.


**Usage**

- **Who** — Subject — replaces 'He/She' in the sentence.
- **Whom** — Object — replaces 'Him/Her' in the sentence.


**Examples**

- ✓ Who called you? (He called → Who)
- ✓ Whom did you call? (You called him → Whom)
- ✗ Whom is calling me? (He is calling = Who)

**Trick:** Replace with He → Who | Replace with Him → Whom


## Rule 10: Fewer vs. Less

**Category:** determiners


Fewer is used with countable nouns (things you can count). Less is used with uncountable nouns (things measured in bulk).


**Usage**

- **Fewer** — Countable nouns — mistakes, students, chairs.
- **Less** — Uncountable nouns — sugar, water, time, effort.


**Examples**

- ✓ She made fewer mistakes in this test.
- ✓ I add less sugar to my tea now.
- ✗ She made less mistakes. (mistakes are countable)

**Trick:** Fewer = Can count | Less = Can't count


## Rule 11: Between vs. Among

**Category:** prepositions


Between is used when referring to exactly two distinct items or people. Among is used when referring to three or more people or a group.


**Usage**

- **Between** — Exactly 2 items or people.
- **Among** — 3 or more — a group or crowd.


**Examples**

- ✓ He divided the money between his two sons.
- ✓ There is a secret among the five friends.
- ✗ He sat between the three chairs.

**Trick:** Between = 2 | Among = 3 or more


## Rule 12: Bring vs. Take

**Category:** confusables


The direction of movement matters. Bring means to carry something toward the speaker. Take means to carry something away from the speaker.


**Usage**

- **Bring** — Movement TOWARD the speaker (come + bring).
- **Take** — Movement AWAY from the speaker (go + take).


**Examples**

- ✓ Bring the book here. (toward me)
- ✓ Take this to school. (away from me)
- ✗ Take your umbrella when you come here.

**Trick:** Come = Bring | Go = Take


## Rule 13: It's vs. Its

**Category:** confusables


It's is a contraction of 'It is' or 'It has'. Its (no apostrophe) is the possessive form, showing that something belongs to 'it'.


**Usage**

- **It's** — Contraction = 'It is' or 'It has'.
- **Its** — Possessive — belonging to it (no apostrophe).


**Examples**

- ✓ It's raining outside. (It is raining)
- ✓ The cat licked its paw. (belonging to the cat)
- ✗ The dog wagged it's tail.

**Trick:** Apostrophe = It is | No apostrophe = Belonging


## Rule 14: Affect vs. Effect

**Category:** confusables


Affect is usually a verb meaning 'to influence'. Effect is usually a noun meaning 'the result or outcome'.


**Usage**

- **Affect** — Verb — to influence or have an impact on.
- **Effect** — Noun — the result, outcome, or consequence.


**Examples**

- ✓ The news affected me deeply. (verb)
- ✓ It had a bad effect on my mood. (noun)
- ✗ The medicine had a good affect.

**Trick:** Affect = Action (verb) | Effect = End result (noun)


## Rule 15: Than vs. Then

**Category:** confusables


Than is used for comparisons. Then refers to time — what happened next, or at a particular time.


**Usage**

- **Than** — Comparison — used with comparatives.
- **Then** — Time — 'next', 'after that', 'at that time'.


**Examples**

- ✓ She is taller than me.
- ✓ Finish this, then go.
- ✗ He is smarter then his brother.

**Trick:** Th-a-n = Compare | Th-e-n = Time


## Rule 16: Either vs. Neither

**Category:** determiners


Both refer to two things or people. Either means one or the other (positive choice). Neither means not one and not the other (negative).


**Usage**

- **Either** — One of two — any one (positive option).
- **Neither** — None of two — not one, not the other (negative).


**Examples**

- ✓ Either option works for me.
- ✓ Neither boy came to the party.
- ✗ Neither of the options are correct. → is correct

**Trick:** Either = One | Neither = None of two


## Rule 17: May vs. Might

**Category:** modals


Both express possibility, but they differ in the degree of likelihood. May shows higher probability; Might shows lower probability or more uncertainty.


**Usage**

- **May** — Higher possibility — about 70% likely.
- **Might** — Lower possibility — about 30% likely.


**Examples**

- ✓ It may rain today. (quite likely)
- ✓ He might come. (less certain)
- ✓ She might have told the truth. (past possibility)

**Trick:** May = 70% chance | Might = 30% chance


## Rule 18: Lie vs. Lay

**Category:** confusables


Lie means to rest or recline (no object needed). Lay means to put something down (needs an object). These are frequently confused — especially in past tense.


**Usage**

- **Lie** — To rest — no object. (lie, lay, lain)
- **Lay** — To put something — needs an object. (lay, laid, laid)


**Examples**

- ✓ I lie on the bed to rest.
- ✓ I laid the book on the table yesterday.
- ✓ Let him lie down for a while.
- ✗ He lies the phone on the table.

**Trick:** Lie = No object (rest) | Lay = Needs object (put)


## Rule 19: Advice vs. Advise

**Category:** confusables


Advice is a noun — the recommendation itself. Advise is a verb — the act of giving advice. The pronunciation difference helps: advice (s-sound) vs. advise (z-sound).


**Usage**

- **Advice** — Noun — a piece of advice, good advice.
- **Advise** — Verb — to advise someone, I advise you.


**Examples**

- ✓ Give me some advice. (noun)
- ✓ I advise you to study hard. (verb)
- ✗ He gave me a great advise.

**Trick:** Noun = Advice | Verb = Advise (like practice/practise)


## Rule 20: Their / There / They're

**Category:** confusables


Three homophones (same sound, different meaning). Context determines which one to use.


**Usage**

- **Their** — Possessive — belonging to them.
- **There** — Place — at that location.
- **They're** — Contraction — 'They are'.


**Examples**

- ✓ Their house is very big.
- ✓ Go there and wait.
- ✓ They're planning their vacation over there.
- ✗ There coming tomorrow.

**Trick:** Their = Belonging | There = Place | They're = They are


## Rule 21: Too vs. Very

**Category:** style


Too implies excess — something is beyond an acceptable limit, often with a negative consequence. Very is simply an intensifier with no negative connotation.


**Usage**

- **Too** — Excess — beyond the acceptable limit (negative consequence).
- **Very** — High degree — just intensifies the adjective (neutral/positive).


**Examples**

- ✓ The tea is too hot to drink. (can't drink it)
- ✓ The movie was very interesting!
- ✗ The tea is very hot to drink.

**Trick:** Too = Over the limit (problem!) | Very = Just strong (okay)


## Rule 22: Beside vs. Besides

**Category:** prepositions


Beside means physically next to something. Besides means in addition to something. One letter makes a huge difference in meaning.


**Usage**

- **Beside** — Next to — physical position.
- **Besides** — In addition to — extra information.


**Examples**

- ✓ Sit beside me. (next to me)
- ✓ Besides math, I also love science. (in addition)
- ✗ He sat besides me for dinner.

**Trick:** Beside = Next to (think: side by side) | Besides = Plus / Also


## Rule 23: Farther vs. Further

**Category:** confusables


Farther refers to physical, measurable distance. Further refers to figurative extension — more of something abstract like discussion, education, or explanation.


**Usage**

- **Farther** — Physical, literal distance you can measure.
- **Further** — Figurative — more, additional, deeper.


**Examples**

- ✓ We walked farther than usual today.
- ✓ No further discussion is needed.
- ✓ We need further clarification.
- ✗ He threw the ball further than me.

**Trick:** Farther = Physical far | Further = Think deeper / more


## Rule 24: Despite vs. In Spite Of

**Category:** conjunctions


Both mean the same thing ('even though') and are followed by a noun or gerund (-ing word). The key difference is structural: Despite has no 'of'; In Spite Of requires 'of'.


**Usage**

- **Despite** — No 'of' → Despite + noun/gerund.
- **In spite of** — Requires 'of' → In spite of + noun/gerund.


**Examples**

- ✓ Despite the rain, we played.
- ✓ In spite of being tired, he worked.
- ✗ Despite of the cold, I wore shorts.

**Trick:** No 'of' after Despite | 'of' is required in In spite of


## Rule 25: Although / Though / Even Though

**Category:** conjunctions


All three are conjunctions showing contrast ('but / however'). They vary in strength. Each must be followed by a full clause (subject + verb). Don't add 'but' or 'yet' after them.


**Usage**

- **Although** — Standard contrast — followed by full clause.
- **Though** — Informal version of although; can end a sentence.
- **Even though** — Strongest contrast — used for surprising facts.


**Examples**

- ✓ Although it was raining, we went out.
- ✓ Even though he is rich, he is humble.
- ✗ Although he is old, but he runs fast.

**Trick:** Even though > Although > Though (in strength of contrast)


## Rule 26: While vs. When

**Category:** conjunctions


While is used for actions that span a duration of time (ongoing). When is used for a specific moment or point in time.


**Usage**

- **While** — Duration — two actions happening at the same time.
- **When** — A specific moment — one action at a precise point.


**Examples**

- ✓ I was studying while it rained. (parallel ongoing)
- ✓ Call me when you reach. (specific moment)
- ✓ I met her when she was shopping.

**Trick:** While = Time span (both happening) | When = Pinpoint moment


## Rule 27: Unless vs. If Not

**Category:** conjunctions


Unless already means 'if not', so never use 'not' with unless — it creates a double negative. Always use the positive form of the verb after unless.


**Usage**

- **Unless** — = 'If not' — negative condition without 'not'.
- **If not** — = Can be used separately but never combine with 'unless'.


**Examples**

- ✓ Unless you study, you will fail.
- ✓ You can't enter unless you show ID.
- ✗ Unless you don't study, you will fail. (double negative)

**Trick:** Unless is already negative — never add 'not' after it!


## Rule 28: Because / Since / As

**Category:** conjunctions


All three show cause or reason, but differ in strength and usage. Because gives the strongest, most direct reason. Since and As suggest the reason is already known.


**Usage**

- **Because** — Strong, direct reason — often the main focus.
- **Since** — Reason is known/secondary — also has a time sense.
- **As** — Soft reason — both things happening at the same time.


**Examples**

- ✓ He left because he was ill. (main reason)
- ✓ Since you're here, let's begin. (known reason)
- ✓ As it was late, we stayed.

**Trick:** Because = Strong reason | Since/As = Soft / known reason


## Rule 29: Due To vs. Because Of

**Category:** prepositions


Both show reason, but their grammatical positions differ. Due to follows a 'be' verb and modifies a noun. Because of introduces the reason before the main action.


**Usage**

- **Due to** — After a 'be' verb (is, was, were) — modifies a noun.
- **Because of** — Before an action verb — introduces the reason.


**Examples**

- ✓ His absence was due to illness. (after 'was')
- ✓ He missed class because of illness. (before action)
- ✗ He left due to he was sick.

**Trick:** Due to = Comes after is/was | Because of = Before the action


## Rule 30: Who vs. That (Relative Clauses)

**Category:** pronouns


Who is exclusively for people. That works as a universal connector for people, animals, and things. Use 'who' when you want to specifically humanize.


**Usage**

- **Who** — Only for people — person-specific.
- **That** — Universal — people, animals, things.


**Examples**

- ✓ The girl who sang won the prize.
- ✓ The singer that impressed us was her.
- ✓ The car that I drive is electric.

**Trick:** That = Universal connector | Who = Person-specific only


## Rule 31: Each Other vs. One Another

**Category:** pronouns


Each other is used when exactly two people or things are involved in a mutual action. One another is used when three or more are involved.


**Usage**

- **Each other** — Exactly 2 people — mutual action between two.
- **One another** — 3 or more people — mutual action in a group.


**Examples**

- ✓ Ram and Shyam respect each other.
- ✓ The team members helped one another.
- ✓ Friends must support one another.

**Trick:** 2 = Each other | 3+ = One another


## Rule 32: Hard vs. Hardly

**Category:** confusables


Hard means with great effort or intensity (works as both adjective and adverb). Hardly is a negative adverb meaning 'barely' or 'almost not at all'.


**Usage**

- **Hard** — Adverb — with effort, intensely, diligently.
- **Hardly** — Negative adverb — barely, almost not, scarcely.


**Examples**

- ✓ She works hard. (with effort)
- ✓ I hardly know him. (barely)
- ✗ He works hardly every day.

**Trick:** Hard = A lot of effort | Hardly = Barely / almost not


## Rule 33: Since vs. Because

**Category:** conjunctions


When used as conjunctions of reason, Since implies the reason is already known to both parties (less emphasis). Because gives new, direct, or emphatic reason.


**Usage**

- **Since** — Reason is known or background — casual.
- **Because** — Clear, strong, or new reason — direct.


**Examples**

- ✓ Since you're here, let's talk.
- ✓ He came because he was invited.
- ✓ I couldn't come because I was busy.

**Trick:** Use Since when the cause is known or secondary


## Rule 34: Not Only…But Also

**Category:** style


Not only…but also is a correlative conjunction pair. For correct grammar, ensure both parts have the same grammatical structure (parallel structure).


**Usage**

- **Structure** — Both sides must match — noun:noun OR verb:verb OR adjective:adjective.


**Examples**

- ✓ She is not only smart but also hardworking. (adj:adj)
- ✓ He not only sings but also dances. (verb:verb)
- ✗ Not only he sings, but also dances.

**Trick:** Balance both sides — Verb:Verb OR Noun:Noun (parallel structure)


## Rule 35: So That vs. In Order That

**Category:** conjunctions


Both express purpose — why something is done. They are followed by a subject + modal verb (can, may, might, could, would).


**Usage**

- **So that** — Common — followed by subject + modal verb.
- **In order that** — Formal — same meaning, more literary.


**Examples**

- ✓ He studies so that he can pass.
- ✓ She trained in order that she may win.
- ✓ I left early so that I could catch the bus.

**Trick:** Both show purpose → follow with can/may/might/could


## Rule 36: Even If vs. Even Though

**Category:** conjunctions


Even if introduces a hypothetical or imaginary condition (might not happen). Even though introduces a real, factual condition (it actually happened or is happening).


**Usage**

- **Even if** — Imaginary / conditional — may or may not happen.
- **Even though** — Reality / factual — it actually is or was true.


**Examples**

- ✓ I'll come even if it rains. (condition — might rain)
- ✓ I came even though it was raining. (reality — it was raining)
- ✓ I'll go even if it rains.

**Trick:** Even if = Condition (hypothetical) | Even though = Fact (reality)


## Rule 37: Unless vs. Until

**Category:** conjunctions


Unless is about condition (if not this, then that). Until is about time (up to a certain point in time). These are completely different words often confused.


**Usage**

- **Unless** — Condition — 'If not'. Subject: will/won't happen.
- **Until** — Time — 'up to the point when'. Ongoing till a moment.


**Examples**

- ✓ Unless you study, you'll fail. (condition)
- ✓ Wait here until I come. (time)
- ✗ Wait here unless I come.

**Trick:** Unless = Condition (If not) | Until = Time (Till that moment)


## Rule 38: Too…To vs. So…That

**Category:** style


Too…to expresses excess that prevents an action — negative outcome implied. So…that shows the degree of something and its actual result.


**Usage**

- **Too…to** — Too + adj + to + verb = Can't do it (negative).
- **So…that** — So + adj + that + result = It happened (positive/neutral).


**Examples**

- ✓ He is too weak to walk. (can't walk)
- ✓ He was so weak that he fell. (did fall)
- ✓ She is too tired to continue.

**Trick:** Too…to = Can't do it | So…that = Did happen (result)


## Rule 39: As If vs. As Though

**Category:** conjunctions


Both As if and As though mean 'in a way that suggests' — they introduce unreal or imaginary situations. Use past tense after them even for present scenarios.


**Usage**

- **As if / As though** — Both express unreal appearance or imagination. Use past tense after them.


**Examples**

- ✓ He behaves as if he were a king. (imaginary)
- ✓ She talks as though she knew everything.
- ✗ He acts as if he is the boss. (should be: were)

**Trick:** Both express false/imaginary situations → Always use past tense after them


## Rule 40: No Sooner…Than

**Category:** tenses


No sooner…than expresses that one action happened immediately after another. It requires subject-auxiliary inversion — the helping verb comes before the subject.


**Usage**

- **Structure** — No sooner + had + subject + V3 + than + V2 (past)


**Examples**

- ✓ No sooner had he arrived than it started raining.
- ✓ No sooner had I called than she cried.
- ✗ No sooner I had called than she cried.

**Trick:** No sooner needs inversion: had + subject + V3 + than


## Rule 41: Scarcely / Hardly / Barely…When

**Category:** tenses


Like 'No sooner…than', these words also show two quick successive actions. They require subject-auxiliary inversion and are always paired with 'when' (not 'than').


**Usage**

- **Structure** — Hardly/Scarcely/Barely + had + subject + V3 + when + V2


**Examples**

- ✓ Hardly had she left when he arrived.
- ✓ Scarcely had I reached when it started.
- ✗ Hardly he had entered when the lights went off.

**Trick:** Hardly/Scarcely/Barely → when (inversion: had + subject + V3)


## Rule 42: So vs. Such

**Category:** style


So intensifies adjectives and adverbs directly. Such intensifies a noun phrase (adjective + noun) or a noun alone.


**Usage**

- **So** — So + adjective/adverb.
- **Such** — Such + (a/an) + adjective + noun.


**Examples**

- ✓ She is so beautiful.
- ✓ It was such a beautiful day.
- ✓ He is so smart. / It was such a good movie.
- ✗ It was so a beautiful day.

**Trick:** So + adjective | Such + noun phrase


## Rule 43: As vs. Like

**Category:** conjunctions


As shows the actual role someone plays (used with subject + verb). Like shows similarity or comparison (used with nouns/pronouns, no verb).


**Usage**

- **As** — Actual role — followed by subject + verb.
- **Like** — Similarity — followed by noun/pronoun only.


**Examples**

- ✓ He works as a teacher. (that IS his role)
- ✓ He sings like a professional. (comparison)
- ✗ Do as I say, not like I say.

**Trick:** As = Actual role | Like = Similar to (comparison)


## Rule 44: All vs. Whole

**Category:** determiners


All is used with plural or uncountable nouns to mean 'every one of'. Whole is used with singular countable nouns to mean 'complete/entire'.


**Usage**

- **All** — Plural / uncountable — all students, all water.
- **Whole** — Singular countable — the whole class, the whole apple.


**Examples**

- ✓ All people were silent.
- ✓ The whole class was present.
- ✓ I ate all the apples. / I ate the whole apple.

**Trick:** All = Many/total | Whole = One complete unit


## Rule 45: One of the + Plural Noun + Singular Verb

**Category:** agreement


When the subject is 'One of the + plural noun', the verb must be singular because it agrees with 'one', not with the plural noun.


**Usage**

- **Rule** — One of the [plural noun] + singular verb (has, is, was, does)


**Examples**

- ✓ One of the boys has won.
- ✓ One of the best books I've read.
- ✗ One of the boys have won.

**Trick:** 'One of' = The subject is ONE → always use singular verb


## Rule 46: The Number vs. A Number

**Category:** agreement


'The number' acts as a singular subject. 'A number of' acts as a plural subject. One article makes all the difference!


**Usage**

- **The number** — Singular — use 'is, was, has'.
- **A number of** — Plural — use 'are, were, have'.


**Examples**

- ✓ The number of students is high.
- ✓ A number of students are missing.
- ✗ A number of students is present.

**Trick:** The number = is (singular) | A number = are (plural)


## Rule 47: Neither of / Either of → Singular

**Category:** agreement


Even though 'of' is followed by a plural noun, the verbs after neither of and either of must always be singular because neither/either is singular.


**Usage**

- **Rule** — Neither of / Either of + plural noun → singular verb


**Examples**

- ✓ Neither of the boys is present.
- ✓ Either of the options is fine.
- ✗ Neither of the girls are dancing.

**Trick:** Neither/Either refers to ONE → always singular verb


## Rule 48: One / None — Singular or Plural?

**Category:** agreement


One is always singular. None is flexible — singular when referring to uncountable nouns, but can be plural when referring to countable/people.


**Usage**

- **One** — Always singular.
- **None** — Usually singular (uncountable); can be plural (countable/people).


**Examples**

- ✓ One of them is coming.
- ✓ None of the food is left. (uncountable)
- ✓ None of the students are present. (countable — acceptable)

**Trick:** One = Always singular | None = Both accepted, context matters


## Rule 49: Each of + Plural Noun = Singular Verb

**Category:** agreement


Even though the noun after 'each of' is plural, the verb must be singular. 'Each' focuses on individuals — one at a time.


**Usage**

- **Rule** — Each of + plural noun → singular verb (is, has, was)


**Examples**

- ✓ Each of the girls is dancing.
- ✓ Each of the boys has done well.
- ✗ Each of the players are excited.

**Trick:** Each of = Think about one at a time → singular verb


## Rule 50: Relative Pronoun Agreement

**Category:** agreement


When a relative pronoun (who, which, that) is used, its verb must agree with the noun it refers to (the antecedent) — not with the pronoun itself.


**Usage**

- **Rule** — Check what the relative pronoun refers to → match verb to that noun.


**Examples**

- ✓ She is one of the girls who dance well. (girls → plural → dance)
- ✓ It is I who am responsible. (I → am)
- ✗ She is one of the girls who dances well.

**Trick:** Check what 'who/which/that' refers to → match verb with that noun


## Rule 51: Not Only…But Also — Parallel Structure

**Category:** style


The correlative conjunction Not only…but also requires the same grammatical structure on both sides — this is called parallel structure.


**Usage**

- **Rule** — Match grammar on both sides: verb:verb, noun:noun, adj:adj.


**Examples**

- ✓ Not only is she smart, but she is also kind. (adj:adj)
- ✓ He not only sings but also dances. (verb:verb)
- ✗ Not only he sings, but also dances.

**Trick:** Balance both sides: Verb+Verb OR Noun+Noun


## Rule 52: As Well As — Verb Agreement

**Category:** agreement


As well as does not create a compound subject. The verb agrees ONLY with the first (main) subject, ignoring the phrase introduced by 'as well as'.


**Usage**

- **Rule** — Verb agrees with first subject only. The 'as well as' phrase is parenthetical.


**Examples**

- ✓ Ram, as well as his friends, is coming. (Ram = singular)
- ✓ She as well as her sisters is beautiful.
- ✗ Ram as well as his friends are coming.

**Trick:** Ignore 'as well as ...' → Verb agrees with the first subject only


## Rule 53: Rather Than — Parallel Structure

**Category:** style


Rather than requires the same form (verb form, tense) on both sides of the comparison. Keep both sides parallel for correct grammar.


**Usage**

- **Rule** — Same tense/form on both sides of 'rather than'.


**Examples**

- ✓ I prefer tea rather than coffee. (noun:noun)
- ✓ He works rather than plays. (verb:verb)
- ✗ I would rather sleeping home than going out.

**Trick:** Use same tense/form on both sides of 'rather than'


## Rule 54: No Sooner…Than — Inversion

**Category:** tenses


When the sentence begins with 'No sooner', subject-auxiliary inversion is required. The helping verb 'had' comes before the subject.


**Usage**

- **Structure** — No sooner + had + subject + V3 + than + past verb


**Examples**

- ✓ No sooner had I reached than the train left.
- ✓ No sooner had he come than it rained.
- ✗ No sooner I reached than the train left.

**Trick:** No sooner = use 'had' + subject + V3 + 'than'


## Rule 55: One of the Most + Superlative + Plural Noun

**Category:** style


After 'One of the most/best/worst', always use a plural noun — even though 'one' is singular. The plural noun is the group, and 'one' is chosen from it.


**Usage**

- **Rule** — One of the + superlative + plural noun


**Examples**

- ✓ One of the most intelligent students in the class.
- ✓ One of the best books I've ever read.
- ✗ One of the best book I've read.

**Trick:** Superlative → always followed by plural noun


## Rule 56: So That — Purpose Clause

**Category:** conjunctions


So that introduces a purpose clause. After 'so that', use a subject plus a modal verb (can, may, might, could, would) — never use an infinitive directly.


**Usage**

- **Structure** — So that + subject + can/may/might/could + base verb


**Examples**

- ✓ He studies hard so that he can succeed.
- ✓ She left early so that she could catch the bus.
- ✗ He studies so that succeed.

**Trick:** So that = Purpose → follow with can/may/might/would


## Rule 57: Because Of vs. Due To (Detailed)

**Category:** prepositions


Both show reason, but they attach differently in a sentence. Due to modifies a noun (often after 'be' verbs). Because of shows the cause of an action.


**Usage**

- **Due to** — Comes after a 'be' verb — His failure was due to laziness.
- **Because of** — Comes before an action — He failed because of laziness.


**Examples**

- ✓ He failed because of laziness.
- ✓ His failure was due to laziness.
- ✗ He left due to he was sick.

**Trick:** Due to = Noun cause | Because of = Verb cause


## Rule 58: Each Other vs. One Another (Review)

**Category:** pronouns


A review: Each other for exactly two people involved in a mutual action. One another for groups of three or more.


**Usage**

- **Each other** — 2 people — mutual action between two.
- **One another** — 3+ people — mutual action in a group.


**Examples**

- ✓ Ram and Shyam help each other.
- ✓ We must support one another.
- ✓ The three boys fought one another.

**Trick:** 2 = Each other | 3+ = One another


## Rule 59: Too…To — Inability

**Category:** style


Too…to structure expresses that something is in excess and therefore prevents an action. The result is always negative (inability).


**Usage**

- **Structure** — Too + adjective + to + base verb = cannot do it


**Examples**

- ✓ He is too weak to walk.
- ✓ It's too cold to go outside.
- ✓ She's too tired to continue.
- ✗ He is too much weak to run.

**Trick:** Too + adj + to + verb = Impossible / can't do it


## Rule 60: So…That — Result

**Category:** style


So…that structure shows intensity leading to a specific result. Unlike 'too...to', the result actually happens.


**Usage**

- **Structure** — So + adjective + that + subject + verb (result)


**Examples**

- ✓ He was so tired that he slept instantly.
- ✓ The movie was so boring that I left.
- ✓ It was so hot that I fainted.
- ✗ He was so tired that sleep instantly.

**Trick:** So + adj + that = the result actually happened


## Rule 61: Hardly/Scarcely…When — Inversion

**Category:** tenses


When 'Hardly' or 'Scarcely' begins a sentence, inversion is required: 'had' comes before the subject. Always paired with 'when', not 'than'.


**Usage**

- **Structure** — Hardly/Scarcely + had + subject + V3 + when + V2


**Examples**

- ✓ Hardly had he arrived when it started raining.
- ✓ Scarcely had I slept when the phone rang.
- ✗ Hardly he had entered when the lights went off.

**Trick:** Hardly/Scarcely → inversion: had + subject + V3 + when


## Rule 62: Too Much vs. Much Too

**Category:** style


Though they look similar, their positions differ. Too much comes before nouns. Much too comes before adjectives or adverbs.


**Usage**

- **Too much** — Before nouns — too much noise, too much food.
- **Much too** — Before adjectives/adverbs — much too difficult, much too late.


**Examples**

- ✓ There is too much noise here.
- ✓ This is much too difficult for me.
- ✗ He is too much tired.

**Trick:** Too much + noun | Much too + adjective/adverb


## Rule 63: Lest — Negative Purpose

**Category:** conjunctions


Lest means 'for fear that' or 'in case … not'. It expresses a negative purpose — you do something to prevent something bad. Always use 'should' after lest.


**Usage**

- **Structure** — Lest + subject + should + base verb


**Examples**

- ✓ Walk carefully lest you should fall.
- ✓ Study now lest you should fail.
- ✓ Lest she should fall, I held her hand.
- ✗ Work hard lest you may fail.

**Trick:** Lest = To prevent → always use should + base verb


## Rule 64: Whether vs. If

**Category:** conjunctions


Whether is used when there are two alternatives or options. If is used for conditions — when something depends on something else happening.


**Usage**

- **Whether** — Two alternatives — whether…or not.
- **If** — Condition — if this happens, then that.


**Examples**

- ✓ I don't know whether he'll come or not.
- ✓ If it rains, we'll cancel the trip.
- ✓ We'll go whether it rains or not.
- ✗ I don't know either he'll attend or not.

**Trick:** Whether = Options/alternatives | If = Condition


## Rule 65: Unless vs. Until (Review)

**Category:** conjunctions


A review of two commonly confused words: Unless introduces a condition (if not). Until introduces a point in time (up to when).


**Usage**

- **Unless** — Condition — if not this, then that.
- **Until** — Time — continuing up to a certain moment.


**Examples**

- ✓ You won't pass unless you study.
- ✓ Wait until I return.
- ✗ Wait here unless I come back.

**Trick:** Unless = If not (condition) | Until = Up to that time


## Rule 66: As If / As Though — Past Tense

**Category:** conjunctions


When as if or as though introduces an unreal/imaginary situation, always use past tense — even when referring to the present.


**Usage**

- **Rule** — As if / As though + past tense (even for present unreal situations)


**Examples**

- ✓ He behaves as if he were a king. (imaginary, so 'were')
- ✓ She talks as though she knew everything.
- ✗ He acts as if he is the boss. → should be 'were'

**Trick:** As if / As though + imaginary = always use past tense (were)


## Rule 67: Would Rather / Had Better

**Category:** modals


Would rather expresses preference between two options. Had better gives strong advice — often implying a warning or consequence if not followed.


**Usage**

- **Would rather** — Preference — I would rather stay than go.
- **Had better** — Strong advice/warning — you had better leave now.


**Examples**

- ✓ I would rather rest than work.
- ✓ You had better study now.
- ✓ I'd rather die than beg.
- ✗ He had better don't go now.

**Trick:** Would rather = Choice/preference | Had better = Warning!


## Rule 68: Hard vs. Hardly (Review)

**Category:** confusables


Review: Hard as an adverb means with great effort. Hardly is a negative adverb meaning barely or almost not at all. They are completely opposite in meaning.


**Usage**

- **Hard** — With effort, diligently, intensely.
- **Hardly** — Barely, almost not, scarcely.


**Examples**

- ✓ He works hard every day.
- ✓ He hardly studies at all.
- ✓ I hardly know her.

**Trick:** Hard = পরিশ্রম থেকে | Hardly = কঠিন থেকে / না এর সমান


## Rule 69: Since vs. From

**Category:** prepositions


Since refers to a definite point in the past and is used with perfect tenses. From indicates a starting point — for past or future — and is used with 'to/till/until'.


**Usage**

- **Since** — Point in past, with have/has/had — since Monday, since 2010.
- **From** — Starting point with end time — from 9 AM to 5 PM.


**Examples**

- ✓ I've been here since Monday.
- ✓ The shop is open from 10 AM to 9 PM.
- ✗ I've been here from Monday.

**Trick:** Since + 'have been' | From = Fixed start time (with 'to')


## Rule 70: Each / Every / All — Three-Way

**Category:** determiners


A three-way distinction: Each for individual focus (2 or more), Every for collective group (3 or more), All for the entire total group.


**Usage**

- **Each** — Individual — 2 or more, one at a time.
- **Every** — Group — 3 or more, all together.
- **All** — Total — the entire group as a whole.


**Examples**

- ✓ Each player got a medal.
- ✓ Every student passed the exam.
- ✓ All players were excited.

**Trick:** 2 → Each | 3+ → Every | Whole group → All


## Rule 71: As…As vs. So…As

**Category:** style


In comparisons of equality: as…as is used for positive sentences. In negative comparisons (with 'not'), traditionally not so…as is preferred (though 'not as…as' is also widely accepted).


**Usage**

- **As…as** — Positive comparison — He is as tall as his brother.
- **Not so…as** — Negative comparison — She is not so smart as you.


**Examples**

- ✓ He is as tall as his brother.
- ✓ She is not so smart as you.
- ✓ She is not as smart as you. (also correct)

**Trick:** Positive = as…as | Negative = not so…as (traditionally)


## Rule 72: Each of / Every One of → Singular

**Category:** agreement


Both 'Each of' and 'Every one of' must be followed by singular verbs, even though a plural noun follows 'of'.


**Usage**

- **Rule** — Each of / Every one of + plural noun → singular verb


**Examples**

- ✓ Each of the boys has done well.
- ✓ Every one of them is ready.
- ✗ Each of the girls are dancing.

**Trick:** 'Each of / Every one of' = Always singular verb


## Rule 73: One vs. Ones

**Category:** pronouns


One is used as a substitute for a singular countable noun. Ones substitutes for a plural countable noun. They avoid repetition of the noun.


**Usage**

- **One** — Singular noun substitute.
- **Ones** — Plural noun substitute.


**Examples**

- ✓ I want the red one. (one shirt)
- ✓ These are old pens. Take new ones. (plural)
- ✓ I like this shirt, but I prefer that one.

**Trick:** Singular = one | Plural = ones


## Rule 74: The Number vs. A Number (Review)

**Category:** agreement


Quick review: The number takes a singular verb. A number of takes a plural verb. The article determines everything.


**Usage**

- **The number** — Singular — The number of students is rising.
- **A number of** — Plural — A number of people are missing.


**Examples**

- ✓ The number of students is rising.
- ✓ A number of students are missing.
- ✓ A number of people are present.

**Trick:** The number → is | A number → are


## Rule 75: Both / Either / Neither — Three-Way

**Category:** determiners


A three-way distinction for pairs: Both = together (positive), Either = one of two (positive choice), Neither = none of two (negative).


**Usage**

- **Both** — Two together — positive. Both are here.
- **Either** — Any one of two — positive choice. Either seat is fine.
- **Neither** — None of the two — negative. Neither answer is right.


**Examples**

- ✓ Both brothers are honest.
- ✓ You can take either seat.
- ✓ Neither answer is correct.

**Trick:** Both = 2 | Either = 1 of 2 | Neither = 0 of 2


## Rule 76: Gerunds — Verb as Noun

**Category:** style


Gerunds are formed by adding -ing to a verb. They function as nouns in a sentence — as subjects, objects, or after prepositions.


**Usage**

- **Gerund** — Verb + ing acting as a noun — swimming, reading, cooking.


**Examples**

- ✓ Swimming is good for health. (subject)
- ✓ I enjoy reading. (object)
- ✓ She is fond of singing. (after preposition)

**Trick:** Gerund = Verb + ing → does the job of a NOUN


## Rule 77: Infinitives — To + Verb

**Category:** style


Infinitives (to + base verb) express purpose, intention, or preference. They often come after verbs like want, refuse, agree, plan, hope, decide.


**Usage**

- **Infinitive** — To + base verb — to learn, to come, to study.


**Examples**

- ✓ I want to learn English.
- ✓ He refused to come.
- ✓ I agreed to do the work.

**Trick:** Infinitive = to + base verb → expresses purpose or intention


## Rule 78: Double Negatives — Avoid!

**Category:** style


Using two negatives in one clause creates a double negative, which is grammatically incorrect in standard English. One negative is enough.


**Usage**

- **Wrong** — Don't use: not + nothing, not + nobody, don't + never.
- **Correct** — Use: not + anything, not + anybody, don't + ever.


**Examples**

- ✓ I don't want anything.
- ✓ She didn't see anybody in the room.
- ✗ I don't want nothing. (double negative)

**Trick:** Negative × Negative = Wrong! One negative is always enough.


## Rule 79: Very / Much / Too — Intensifiers

**Category:** style


Each intensifier has a specific use: Very + adjective (neutral intensity), Much + past participle or comparative, Too = excessive (negative consequence).


**Usage**

- **Very** — Very + adjective/adverb — very good, very fast.
- **Much** — Much + past participle or comparative — much improved, much better.
- **Too** — Too + adjective — excess with negative result.


**Examples**

- ✓ Very good work!
- ✓ He is much improved since last month.
- ✓ Too expensive to buy.

**Trick:** Very + adj | Much + V3/comparative | Too = Over-limit


## Rule 80: Past Perfect — Two Past Actions

**Category:** tenses


The past perfect (had + V3) is used for an action that was completed BEFORE another action in the past. The earlier action uses past perfect; the later uses simple past.


**Usage**

- **Structure** — Earlier action: had + V3 | Later action: simple past (V2)


**Examples**

- ✓ He had left before I arrived. (left = earlier)
- ✓ They had finished the game when it rained.
- ✓ When I reached, he had been waiting for an hour.

**Trick:** Past perfect = 'had + V3' → action that happened first in the past


## Rule 81: So That / In Order That — Purpose

**Category:** conjunctions


Both introduce purpose clauses. They explain WHY something is done. Follow them with a subject and modal verb for correct structure.


**Usage**

- **Structure** — So that/In order that + subject + can/may/might/could + verb


**Examples**

- ✓ He studies hard so that he can succeed.
- ✓ She left early in order that she might catch the train.
- ✓ I work hard so that I could save money.

**Trick:** Both = Purpose → always follow with modal verb (can/may/might)


## Rule 82: Despite / Although / However

**Category:** conjunctions


Three different words all showing contrast, but used very differently. Despite = preposition, Although = conjunction (same sentence), However = adverb (separate sentence).


**Usage**

- **Despite** — Preposition + noun/gerund — Despite being tired…
- **Although** — Conjunction + full clause — Although he was tired…
- **However** — Adverb connecting two sentences — He was tired. However, he worked.


**Examples**

- ✓ Despite being tired, he worked.
- ✓ Although he was tired, he worked.
- ✓ He was tired. However, he worked.

**Trick:** Despite + noun/gerund | Although + clause | However = new sentence


## Rule 83: Emphatic 'Do/Does/Did'

**Category:** style


Adding do/does/did to a positive sentence gives emphasis — it stresses that the action truly happened or is true. This is the emphatic use of auxiliaries.


**Usage**

- **Emphatic** — I DO like chocolates. / He DID try his best.


**Examples**

- ✓ I do like chocolates! (emphasis)
- ✓ He did try his best. (past emphasis)
- ✓ She does study hard. (present emphasis)

**Trick:** Add do/does/did to positive sentences for strong emphasis


## Rule 84: Used To vs. Be Used To

**Category:** tenses


Used to describes a past habit that no longer continues. Be used to means to be accustomed to something (comfortable with it now, in the present).


**Usage**

- **Used to** — Past habit no longer true — Used to + base verb.
- **Be used to** — Accustomed to now — be + used to + verb-ing/noun.


**Examples**

- ✓ I used to play cricket. (past habit, now stopped)
- ✓ I am used to getting up early. (accustomed now)
- ✓ I'm used to waking early.

**Trick:** Used to = Past habit | Be used to = Comfortable now


## Rule 85: Neither of / Either of / Both of

**Category:** agreement


Verb rules for these three: 'Neither of' and 'Either of' take singular verbs. 'Both of' takes a plural verb.


**Usage**

- **Neither of** — Singular verb — Neither of the boys has come.
- **Either of** — Singular verb — Either of the answers is correct.
- **Both of** — Plural verb — Both of them are ready.


**Examples**

- ✓ Neither of the boys has come.
- ✓ Either of the answers is correct.
- ✓ Both of them are ready.

**Trick:** Neither/Either of = singular | Both of = plural


## Rule 86: If I Were / If He Were — Hypothetical

**Category:** tenses


In hypothetical or imaginary conditionals (Type 2), use were for ALL subjects — not 'was', even for singular subjects like I, he, she.


**Usage**

- **Rule** — If + subject + were (for all persons — imaginary/unreal)


**Examples**

- ✓ If I were you, I'd study more.
- ✓ If he were rich, he'd travel.
- ✓ He behaves as if he were a king.
- ✗ If I was you, I'd study more.

**Trick:** If + person + were = Imaginary (always 'were', not 'was')


## Rule 87: By / Until / Till — Time Prepositions

**Category:** prepositions


By means before or at a deadline. Until/Till means continuing up to a point in time. 'Until' and 'Till' mean the same thing.


**Usage**

- **By** — Deadline — before or at that time.
- **Until/Till** — Ongoing — up to that moment.


**Examples**

- ✓ Finish it by Monday. (deadline)
- ✓ Wait until I come. (ongoing till that moment)
- ✗ I will wait by you come back.

**Trick:** By = Deadline | Until/Till = Ongoing till a point


## Rule 88: Would Have + V3 — Unreal Past

**Category:** tenses


Would have + V3 is used in Type 3 conditionals (third conditional) to talk about unreal past situations — things that didn't happen but could have.


**Usage**

- **Structure** — If had + V3 → would have + V3


**Examples**

- ✓ I would have helped you, but I was busy.
- ✓ She would have come if she had known.
- ✓ If I had worked hard, I would have passed.

**Trick:** Would have + V3 = Imagined past / things that didn't happen


## Rule 89: Not Only…But Also — Parallel (Review)

**Category:** style


Review: 'Not only…but also' is a correlative pair requiring parallel structure. The grammatical form on both sides must match.


**Usage**

- **Rule** — Match grammar → verb:verb, noun:noun, adj:adj


**Examples**

- ✓ Not only intelligent but also hardworking. (adj:adj)
- ✓ Not only does he dance, but he also sings.
- ✗ Not only he sings, but also dances.

**Trick:** Match grammar before/after → parallel structure


## Rule 90: Much / Very Much / Too Much

**Category:** style


Three related but distinct intensifiers: Much = quantity. Very much = strong emphasis (degree). Too much = excessive (a problem).


**Usage**

- **Much** — Quantity — There isn't much time.
- **Very much** — Intensity — I like it very much.
- **Too much** — Excessive (negative) — He ate too much food.


**Examples**

- ✓ He ate too much food.
- ✓ I like it very much.
- ✓ There isn't much time.

**Trick:** Much = Quantity | Very much = Intensity | Too much = Problem!


## Rule 91: Hard vs. Hardly (Hindi Trick)

**Category:** confusables


Final review with Hindi keywords: Hard = পরিশ্রম থেকে (with effort). Hardly = কঠিন থেকে / না এর সমান (barely/scarcely).


**Usage**

## Rule 92: So vs. Such (Review)

**Category:** style


Review: So comes before adjectives/adverbs alone. Such comes before noun phrases (adjective + noun).


**Usage**

- **So** — So + adj/adverb — He is so smart.
- **Such** — Such + (a/an) + adj + noun — It was such a good movie.


**Examples**

- ✓ He is so smart.
- ✓ It was such a good movie.
- ✓ She such a beautiful singer that she won.
- ✗ It was so a beautiful day.

**Trick:** So + smart | Such + a smart boy


## Rule 93: Much / Many / A Lot Of

**Category:** determiners


Much = uncountable. Many = countable. A lot of = works with BOTH countable and uncountable nouns. When unsure, use 'a lot of'.


**Usage**

- **Much** — Uncountable — much sugar, much water, much time.
- **Many** — Countable — many students, many books, many cars.
- **A lot of** — Both — a lot of money, a lot of students.


**Examples**

- ✓ Much sugar. ✓ Many students. ✓ A lot of money.
- ✓ There are a lot of people outside.
- ✗ How many sugar do you need?

**Trick:** Not sure? Use A lot of — it works everywhere!


## Rule 94: Even / Even Though / Even If

**Category:** conjunctions


Three related phrases: Even = emphasis. Even though = surprising real fact. Even if = imaginary condition.


**Usage**

- **Even** — Emphasis — Even a child knows this.
- **Even though** — Surprising reality — I came even though I was sick.
- **Even if** — Imaginary condition — I'll go even if it rains.


**Examples**

- ✓ Even a child knows this.
- ✓ I came even though I was sick.
- ✓ I'll go even if it rains.

**Trick:** Even = Stress | Even though = Reality | Even if = Condition


## Rule 95: Else / Other / Another

**Category:** determiners


Three words that indicate 'something different': Else = different person/thing. Other = the remaining one(s). Another = one more of the same.


**Usage**

- **Else** — Different person/thing — someone else, somewhere else.
- **Other** — The remaining — the other option, other students.
- **Another** — One more — another pen, another day.


**Examples**

- ✓ Someone else called.
- ✓ The other option is better.
- ✓ Give me another pen.

**Trick:** Another = Extra one | Other = Different remaining | Else = Different person/thing


## Rule 96: Hope vs. Wish

**Category:** confusables


Hope is used for real, possible situations in the future. Wish is used for unreal, impossible, or regretful situations (contrary to fact).


**Usage**

- **Hope** — Real possibility — I hope you pass.
- **Wish** — Unreal / impossible — I wish I were taller.


**Examples**

- ✓ I hope you pass. (it's possible)
- ✓ I wish I were taller. (I'm not, unreal)
- ✓ I wish I had studied harder. (regret about past)

**Trick:** Hope = Future possible | Wish = Unreal / regret


## Rule 97: Too vs. Enough

**Category:** style


Too means more than required — excess, negative outcome. Enough means sufficient — just the right amount, positive outcome.


**Usage**

- **Too** — Excess — too + adjective + to + verb (can't do it).
- **Enough** — Sufficient — adjective + enough + to + verb (can do it).


**Examples**

- ✓ He is too tired to work. (can't work)
- ✓ She is smart enough to win. (can win)
- ✓ He is strong enough to lift it.

**Trick:** Too = Extra (Problem!) | Enough = Just right (Good)


## Rule 98: Gerunds vs. Infinitives — Verb Choice

**Category:** style


Some verbs are always followed by gerunds, some by infinitives. Knowing which verb takes which form prevents common errors.


**Usage**

- **Gerund after** — enjoy, avoid, mind, suggest, finish, deny, consider.
- **Infinitive after** — want, plan, hope, agree, refuse, decide, manage.


**Examples**

- ✓ I enjoy swimming. (enjoy + gerund)
- ✓ I want to swim. (want + infinitive)
- ✓ She suggested leaving early.

**Trick:** Enjoy/avoid/suggest → Gerund | Want/plan/hope → Infinitive


## Rule 99: Due To vs. Because Of (Review)

**Category:** prepositions


Final review: Both show reason, but 'Due to' is more formal and follows 'be' verbs. 'Because of' is used before the action in casual or direct causation.


**Usage**

- **Due to** — Formal — caused by (after is/was).
- **Because of** — Casual — reason (before action verb).


**Examples**

- ✓ The flight was delayed due to rain.
- ✓ He failed because of laziness.
- ✗ He left due to he was sick.

**Trick:** 'Due to' = Formal (after be verb) | 'Because of' = Casual (reason)


## Rule 100: Each Other vs. One Another (Final)

**Category:** pronouns

**Usage**

- ✗ The three boys fought each other.

## Rule 101: Sometime / Sometimes / Some Time

**Category:** confusables


Three different words, all pronounced similarly. Each has a distinct meaning: unspecified time, occasionally, or a period of time.


**Usage**

**Trick:** Sometime = কখন? | Sometimes = মাঝে মাঝে | Some time = সামান্য সময়


## Rule 102: Since vs. From (Review)

**Category:** prepositions


Review: Since marks a point in the past and is used with perfect tenses. From marks a starting point and needs a paired 'to/till/until'.


**Usage**

- **Since** — Past point → to now — with have/has/had.
- **From** — Start point + end — from 9 to 5, from Monday to Friday.


**Examples**

- ✓ I have lived here since 2010.
- ✓ I worked from 9 AM to 5 PM.
- ✗ I've been here from Monday.

**Trick:** Since = Past → Now (with perfect tense) | From = Start + end time


## Rule 103: All vs. Whole (Review)

**Category:** determiners


Review: All is used with plural or uncountable nouns. Whole is used with a singular countable noun to mean 'entire/complete'.


**Usage**

- **All** — Plural or total — all the apples, all the people.
- **Whole** — One complete unit — the whole apple, the whole day.


**Examples**

- ✓ I ate all the apples.
- ✓ I ate the whole apple.
- ✓ All the students passed.

**Trick:** Whole = One complete item | All = Many or the total


## Rule 104: Hardly / Barely / Scarcely — Negative Adverbs

**Category:** style


All three mean 'almost not' and carry negative meaning. Never combine them with other negatives (not, no, never) — it creates double negatives.


**Usage**

- **Hardly** — Almost not — I could hardly walk.
- **Barely** — Almost not — He barely passed.
- **Scarcely** — Almost not — We scarcely met.


**Examples**

- ✓ I could hardly walk.
- ✓ He barely passed the exam.
- ✓ We scarcely met.
- ✗ I couldn't hardly walk. (double negative)

**Trick:** All three = Negative → avoid double negatives with them


## Rule 105: That vs. Which — Essential vs. Extra Info

**Category:** pronouns


That introduces essential (defining) information — removing it changes the meaning. Which introduces extra (non-defining) information — set off by commas.


**Usage**

- **That** — Essential info — no commas. The car that I bought is red.
- **Which** — Extra info — with commas. The car, which I bought last year, is red.


**Examples**

- ✓ The car that I bought is red. (essential — which car)
- ✓ The car, which I bought last year, is red. (extra info)
- ✗ The car which I bought is red. (no comma — should be 'that')

**Trick:** That = Essential (no comma) | Which = Extra (use comma)


## Rule 106: Yet / Still / Already

**Category:** style


Three time adverbs often confused: Still = something continuing. Yet = something not happened (in questions/negatives). Already = something happened before expected.


**Usage**

- **Still** — Continuing — She is still sleeping.
- **Yet** — Not happened (questions/negatives) — Have you eaten yet?
- **Already** — Happened before expected — I've already done it.


**Examples**

- ✓ She is still sleeping.
- ✓ Have you eaten yet?
- ✓ I've already done it.
- ✓ He hasn't arrived yet.

**Trick:** Still = এখনো ও | Yet = এখন পর্যন্ত না | Already = আগে থেকে হয় গেছে


## Rule 107: Few / Less / Several

**Category:** determiners


Three quantity words: Few = countable (negative tone). Several = countable (more than a few, neutral/positive). Less = uncountable.


**Usage**

- **Few** — Countable, negative — Few people came.
- **Several** — Countable, more than few — Several students passed.
- **Less** — Uncountable — Less water is left.


**Examples**

- ✓ Few people came. (almost none)
- ✓ Several students passed. (more than a few)
- ✓ Less water is left.

**Trick:** Few (in amount) | Less = Can't count


## Rule 108: Whoever vs. Whomever

**Category:** pronouns


Whoever is the subject form. Whomever is the object form. Apply the He/Him test: if 'He' fits, use Whoever; if 'Him' fits, use Whomever.


**Usage**

- **Whoever** — Subject (He) — Whoever comes is welcome.
- **Whomever** — Object (Him) — Invite whomever you want.


**Examples**

- ✓ Whoever comes is welcome. (He comes = Whoever)
- ✓ Invite whomever you want. (You want him = Whomever)

**Trick:** He = Whoever | Him = Whomever


## Rule 109: When / While / As — Time Conjunctions

**Category:** conjunctions


Three time conjunctions: When = single/specific action, While = two ongoing parallel actions, As = simultaneous actions (often cause-and-effect).


**Usage**

- **When** — Specific moment — I was sleeping when he called.
- **While** — Parallel ongoing — I was cooking while she was studying.
- **As** — Simultaneous — As I walked in, she left.


**Examples**

- ✓ I was sleeping when he called.
- ✓ I was cooking while she was studying.
- ✓ As I walked in, she left.

**Trick:** When = One event | While = Parallel actions | As = Simultaneous


## Rule 110: At / In / On — Time Prepositions

**Category:** prepositions


The three main time prepositions: At for exact times, On for days and dates, In for months, years, seasons, and longer periods.


**Usage**

- **At** — Exact time — at 5 PM, at noon, at midnight.
- **On** — Days and dates — on Monday, on 15th August.
- **In** — Months, years, seasons — in March, in 2024, in winter.


**Examples**

- ✓ At 5 PM. ✓ On Monday. ✓ In March.
- ✓ I was born in 1999.
- ✓ The train arrives at 8 AM on Friday.

**Trick:** At = Clock | On = Day/Date | In = Month/Year/Period


## Rule 111: Suppose / Provided / Unless — Conditionals

**Category:** conjunctions


These words all introduce conditions: Suppose/Supposing = imagine this, Provided/Providing = only if, Unless = if not.


**Usage**

- **Suppose** — Imagine a condition — Suppose he comes late, what will you do?
- **Provided** — Only if — Provided you work hard, you'll pass.
- **Unless** — If not — You won't pass unless you study.


**Examples**

- ✓ Suppose he comes late, what will you do?
- ✓ Provided you work hard, you'll pass.
- ✓ You won't pass unless you study.

**Trick:** Suppose = Imagine | Provided = Only if | Unless = If not


## Rule 112: Much Too vs. Too Much (Review)

**Category:** style


Review: Much too comes before adjectives/adverbs. Too much comes before nouns. Position determines which to use.


**Usage**

- **Much too** — Before adjective/adverb — He is much too lazy.
- **Too much** — Before noun — I ate too much food.


**Examples**

- ✓ He is much too lazy.
- ✓ I ate too much food.
- ✗ He is too much lazy.

**Trick:** Much too + Adj | Too much + Noun


## Rule 113: So That / In Order That (Review)

**Category:** conjunctions


Review: Both mean 'for the purpose of'. Always follow with a modal verb (can, could, may, might) and then the base verb.


**Usage**

- **Structure** — So that / In order that + subject + modal (can/may/could/might) + verb


**Examples**

- ✓ I studied so that I could pass.
- ✓ She left early in order that she might catch the train.
- ✓ He works hard so that he can succeed.

**Trick:** Use modal can/could/may/might after 'so that/in order that'


## Rule 114: In Case vs. If

**Category:** conjunctions


In case = taking precaution against something that might happen. If = a condition — something happens BECAUSE of something else.


**Usage**

- **In case** — Precaution — take umbrella in case it rains (it might).
- **If** — Condition — I'll go if he comes (only then).


**Examples**

- ✓ Take an umbrella in case it rains. (precaution)
- ✓ I'll go if he comes. (condition)
- ✗ I'll take an umbrella if it may rain.

**Trick:** In case = Precaution | If = Possibility/Condition


## Rule 115: As If / As Though (Review)

**Category:** conjunctions


Review: Both 'as if' and 'as though' are used for unreal/imaginative situations. They are interchangeable and always followed by past tense.


**Usage**

- **As if / As though** — Unreal/imaginative + past tense


**Examples**

- ✓ He talks as if he were a king.
- ✓ She smiled as though nothing had happened.
- ✗ He acts as if he is the boss.

**Trick:** Use past tense after 'as if / as though' for unreal things


## Rule 116: Had Better / Would Rather (Review)

**Category:** modals


Review: Had better gives strong advice or warning (there will be a consequence). Would rather expresses preference between two choices.


**Usage**

- **Had better** — Strong warning/advice — You had better study now.
- **Would rather** — Preference — I would rather sleep than work.


**Examples**

- ✓ You had better study now. (warning)
- ✓ I would rather stay home than go out. (preference)
- ✗ He had better don't go now.

**Trick:** Had better = Warning | Would rather = Choice


## Rule 117: Because vs. So — Not Together!

**Category:** conjunctions


Never use 'because' and 'so' in the same sentence — they both show cause and result. Use one or the other, not both, as it creates a redundant double connector.


**Usage**

- **Wrong** — He was tired because he was ill, so he slept.
- **Correct** — He was tired because he was ill. OR: He was ill, so he slept.


**Examples**

- ✓ He was tired because he was ill.
- ✓ He was ill, so he slept.
- ✗ He was tired because he was ill, so he slept.

**Trick:** Use either Because OR So — NEVER both in one sentence!


## Rule 118: No Sooner…Than / Hardly…When (Review)

**Category:** tenses


Review: Two quick actions in rapid succession. 'No sooner' pairs with 'than'; 'Hardly/Scarcely' pairs with 'when'. Both require subject-auxiliary inversion with 'had'.


**Usage**

- **No sooner…than** — No sooner had + subject + V3 + than + V2
- **Hardly…when** — Hardly had + subject + V3 + when + V2


**Examples**

- ✓ No sooner had he come than it rained.
- ✓ Hardly had I slept when the phone rang.
- ✗ Hardly had he arrived than it started.

**Trick:** No sooner → than | Hardly → when (inversion with 'had')


## Rule 119: Each Other vs. One Another (Three-Peat)

**Category:** pronouns


One more review to cement the rule: 'Each other' is strictly for two. 'One another' is for three or more. This is a common exam question.


**Usage**

- **Each other** — 2 people — Ram and Shyam respect each other.
- **One another** — 3+ people — We should all help one another.


**Examples**

- ✓ Ram and Shyam respect each other.
- ✓ We should all help one another.
- ✗ The three friends fought each other.

**Trick:** Each other = 2 | One another = 3+


## Rule 120: Emphatic Pronouns

**Category:** pronouns


Emphatic pronouns (myself, yourself, himself, herself, ourselves, themselves) are used after the subject to add emphasis and stress. They are NOT reflexive here — they emphasize, not reflect the action.


**Usage**

- **Emphatic** — I myself, she herself, he himself — used after subject for stress.
- **Reflexive** — She hurt herself — the subject receives the action.


**Examples**

- ✓ I myself completed the work. (emphasis)
- ✓ She herself cooked the food.
- ✓ The principal himself took the class.
- ✗ I myself was hurt. (reflexive, not emphatic)

**Trick:** Emphatic pronouns come after the subject to add stress — not as an object
