## Rule 1: Since / For

**Topic:** Prepositions of Time with Perfect Tenses


'Since' is used with a point of time (since 2002, since Monday, since he left) while 'for' is used with a length or period of time (for three years, for two hours). Both normally require a perfect or perfect continuous tense, not a simple present/present continuous or simple past. Expressions like 'from the past two weeks' or 'by three years' are wrong; use 'for the past two weeks' and 'for three years'.


**Examples**

- ✗ They have lived in this apartment since ten years.
  - 'Ten years' is a length of time, so 'for ten years' must be used instead of 'since ten years'.
- ✗ As it has been raining heavily since two hours, the children are at home.
  - 'Two hours' denotes a period of time, so 'for two hours' is correct.
- ✗ Smita lived in this house since she was five years old.
  - With 'since', a perfect tense is required: 'Smita has lived / has been living in this house'.


## Rule 2: Each / Every / Either / Neither / Any / None

**Topic:** Determiners and Pronouns


'Each' works both as an adjective and a pronoun, so 'each of the...' is correct; 'every' is only an adjective and cannot act as a subject, so 'every of the...' is wrong. Each and every are always followed by a singular noun and take a singular verb, along with a singular possessive (his/her, not their). 'Either/neither' refer to two things and 'any/none' to more than two, and 'either of/neither of/any of/none of' must be followed by a plural noun with a singular verb.


**Examples**

- ✗ Every of the five children (1) standing in the corner (2) of the room is dexterous in paintings. (3) No error (4)
  - 'Every' cannot be used as a pronoun; 'Each of the five children' is correct.
- ✗ Every of the two girls will get the reward.
  - 'Every of' is wrong; use 'Each of the two girls'.
- ✗ The essay is divided into five paragraphs and every of these has seven sentences.
  - 'Every of these' should be 'each of these', since 'every' is only an adjective.


## Rule 3: Use of Who / Whom / Whose / Which / That

**Topic:** Relative Pronouns


'Who' is used for persons as the subject of a verb, while 'whom' is used for persons as the object of a verb or preposition. 'Whose' shows possession or ownership, and must not be confused with 'who's', which is the contraction of 'who is / who was / who has'. 'Which' and 'that' are used for things, animals and non-living nouns, so a person should never be referred to with 'which', nor a thing with 'who'.


**Examples**

- ✗ My friend who's leg was fractured, has recovered.
  - Possession requires 'whose leg'; 'who's' means 'who is/has'.
- ✗ What is the name of the Swami whom autobiography impressed you so much?
  - Possession of the autobiography requires 'whose autobiography', not 'whom'.
- ✗ This is the album who I found in the garage.
  - 'Album' is a thing, so the relative pronoun must be 'which/that I found'.


## Rule 4: Modal Verb & Its Uses

**Topic:** Modals


Modal verbs (can, could, may, might, shall, should, will, would, must, ought to, need, dare, used to, had better) are always followed by the bare infinitive (V1) without 'to', except 'ought to', 'used to' and 'have to'. Two modals cannot be used together, and a modal cannot be combined with 'be able to' in the same clause (e.g. 'could not be able to' is wrong). Modals of possibility already carry the sense of probability, so words like 'possibly/likely' must not be added; in purpose clauses 'so that' takes 'may/might/can/could', and 'lest' takes 'should' with no extra negative.


**Examples**

- ✗ Passengers (a) may not smoke (b) inside the plane, (c) no error (d)
  - Prohibition by rule/law requires 'must not' (or 'cannot'), not 'may not'.
- ✗ He is working (a) hard so that he should (b) succeed in his mission, (c) no error (d)
  - 'So that' expressing purpose takes 'may/can' (present context), not 'should'.
- ✗ He expected (a) that the problem will be (b) sorted out very soon, (c) no error (d)
  - Past main verb 'expected' requires 'would be' in the subordinate clause.


## Rule 5: Questions Related to Singular & Plural Noun

**Topic:** Noun (Number)


Certain nouns in English do not change form in the plural or are already plural, so adding '-s' is an error. Words like cattle, furniture, luggage, information, advice, poetry, bricks and equipment are uncountable or collective and take no plural '-s'. Some nouns form plurals irregularly (mice, teeth, oxen, sheep), and in compound nouns the main word is pluralised (mothers-in-law, cupfuls, passers-by, breaks-in).


**Examples**

- ✗ These stray cattles are roaming around the town.
  - 'Cattle' is already plural; 'cattles' is incorrect.
- ✗ This furnitures has been taken on hire for the function in the college.
  - 'Furniture' is an uncountable noun and has no plural form.
- ✗ I think all our furniture needing to be polished.
  - 'Furniture' is uncountable and takes a singular verb: 'needs to be polished'.


## Rule 6: Errors Related to Infinitives

**Topic:** Infinitive / Gerund


Certain verbs and expressions are followed by a gerund (-ing) rather than an infinitive, and vice versa. Verbs like plan for/settle, hope, recall, prohibit from, take care of, cannot help and let take fixed patterns; 'let' and perception verbs such as 'see' take the bare infinitive without 'to'. Choosing the wrong form between 'to + verb' and 'verb + ing' is the typical error tested.


**Examples**

- ✗ They are planning for settling in Mumbai with their son.
  - 'Plan' is followed by the infinitive: 'planning to settle'.
- ✗ We saw (a) him to walk (b) in the park on the way home, (c) No error (d)
  - After the perception verb 'saw', the bare infinitive or '-ing' form is used: 'him walk/walking'.
- ✗ Avika hopes getting a distinction in English.
  - 'Hope' is followed by an infinitive: 'hopes to get'.


## Rule 7: Uses of Gerund

**Topic:** Gerund


A gerund (verb + ing) works as a noun and must be used as the subject of a sentence, after prepositions, and after certain verbs and fixed expressions such as 'with a view to', 'look forward to', 'aim at', 'consider', 'persist in', 'take pride in', 'focus on' and 'addicted to'. Using an infinitive or a bare noun form in these positions is an error. Possessive or objective pronouns before the gerund are also part of this structure.


**Examples**

- ✗ Buy presents for ladies (A) in their absence (B) is a very difficult task. (C) No error (D)
  - The subject of the sentence must be a gerund: 'Buying presents for ladies'.
- ✗ Character build should be an integral part (1) of every aspect of schooling and learning as it (2) equips one to find long-term solutions to life's challenges. (3) No error (4)
  - The gerund is needed: 'Character building should be an integral part'.
- ✗ Be polite and courteous will greatly improve your chances of making friends at your new office.
  - The subject must be a gerund phrase: 'Being polite and courteous will greatly improve...'.


## Rule 8: Uses of Participle

**Topic:** Participle


Present participles (V+ing) describe something that causes an action or feeling and act actively (an amusing story, a frightening film), while past participles (V3) describe the receiver of the action and act passively (an amused girl, a frightened child). Participles used as adjectives must take their correct form (law-abiding citizens, limited passengers, emerging technology, molten/melted gold). The perfect participle is 'having + V3' (Having paid the fee...), and the participle must logically refer to the subject of the main clause, otherwise it becomes a dangling participle.


**Examples**

- ✗ Rashmi was quite amusing when she heard about what had happened.
- ✗ There are just too few trains (a) for the ever grow (b) number of passengers in the city. (c) No Error (d)
- ✗ Many people who are otherwise (1) law-abide citizens (2) break traffic rules knowingly. (3) No error (4)


## Rule 9: Articles: No Article in General Sense

**Topic:** Articles


When a person or thing is spoken of in a general sense, no article is used before it. So abstract nouns, material nouns and generic plural/uncountable nouns take no article (e.g. 'Man is mortal', 'Honesty is the best policy'). Saying 'The man is mortal' or 'The honesty is the best policy' is therefore wrong.


**Examples**

- ✗ One should (a) be true to one's promises (b) in order to earn name and fame in the life (c) No Error (d)
- ✗ The man is mortal (a) and ultimately he has to leave everything here (b) yet how irrationally he remains attached to his wordly possessions (c) No Error (d)
- ✗ He did not go (a) to the city on foot (b) he went there by the train (c) No Error (d)


## Rule 10: Use of Article Before Proper Nouns

**Topic:** Articles


Proper nouns normally take no article, but when a proper noun is used to describe a quality or type, the indefinite article 'a/an' is placed before it (e.g. 'Kalidas is a Shakespeare of India', 'Ahmedabad is becoming a Manchester of India'). Similarly, 'the' is used before a common noun that stands for a quality within a person (e.g. 'The judge in him prevailed upon the father').


**Examples**

- ✗ Ahmedabad is trying (1) to become (2) next Manchester of India (3) No error (4)
- ✗ Kalidas is (a) a Shakespeare (b) of India (c) No error (d)
- ✗ Due to its beauty (a) Kashmir is known as (b) Switzerland of India (c) No error (d)


## Rule 11: Articles with Superlative, Comparative Degrees and Ordinals

**Topic:** Articles


Use 'the' before superlative degrees and ordinal numbers (the best soldier, the first European sailor). 'The' is also used before a comparative when only two persons/things are compared (of the two, she is the better) and in double comparatives (the more he studied, the greater was his fascination). Note that 'a most' + adjective means 'very' and is not a true superlative.


**Examples**

- ✗ A first European sailor (a) who came to India, (b) was Vasco-da-Gama. (c) No error (d)
- ✗ Of the two (a) sisters, she (b) is better, (c) No error (d)
- ✗ He is the best (a) artist of time (b) but unfortunately least recognised (c) No error (d)


## Rule 12: One Article for a Single Entity

**Topic:** Articles


When two nouns or adjectives refer to one and the same person/thing regarded as a whole, the article is used only before the first noun/adjective. Repeating the article before the second noun/adjective makes them two separate persons/things. So 'a singer and scientist' means one person, while 'a singer and a scientist' means two people.


**Examples**

- ✗ On my request (a) Lalit introduced me (b) to his friend who is a singer and a scientist (c) No Error (d)
  - One friend is being described, so a single article is enough: 'a singer and scientist'.
- ✗ Mr. Jha has a white (a) and a black dog (b) which guards his house from robbers (c) No Error (d)
  - Only one dog is meant (verb 'guards' is singular), so the article should not be repeated: 'a white and black dog'.


## Rule 13: Omission of Articles in Fixed Phrases

**Topic:** Articles


Certain set phrases and idiomatic expressions never take an article, e.g. by mistake, in trouble, in conclusion, at the top of one's voice, set on fire, lose heart, fall silent. Adding 'a/an/the' inside such phrases is an error. Some phrases, however, require the article by convention, e.g. 'in a bad mood', 'in a temper'.


**Examples**

- ✗ It was (a) by a mistake (b) that he injured her hand (c) No error (d)
  - The fixed phrase is 'by mistake' — no article.
- ✗ My father is (a) in bad mood (b) today, (c) No error (d)
  - Correct form is 'in a bad mood'.
- ✗ He found himself (a) in a trouble (b) when he saw no rickshaw outside the station (c) No error (d)
  - The phrase is 'in trouble' — no article.


## Rule 14: Uses of Much / Many / Little / Few

**Topic:** Determiners / Quantifiers


'Much' and 'little' are used with uncountable nouns, while 'many' and 'few' are used with countable nouns. 'Little/Few' without an article carry a negative sense (almost none), 'a little/a few' carry a positive sense (at least some), and 'the little/the few' mean whatever small quantity remains. Also note 'much' modifies uncountables and comparatives, not adjectives like 'nice' in the positive degree.


**Examples**

- ✗ Little that I knew of the English language was not sufficient to make me an English teacher. (Substitute the underlined segment 'Little that I knew')
  - 'The little' is used for the small remaining quantity that exists, so 'The little that I knew' is correct.
- ✗ They grasp at the (1) things and avoid (2) a little things (3) No error (4)
  - 'Things' is countable plural, so 'a few things' should be used instead of 'a little things'.
- ✗ Much thanks (a) for your kind message of greetings (b) which I reciprocate most heartily, (c) no error (d)
  - 'Thanks' is plural countable, so 'Many thanks' is correct.


## Rule 15: Many / A Number of / The Number of / More than one

**Topic:** Subject-Verb Agreement


'Many a' and 'more than one' are followed by a singular noun and a singular verb. 'Many', 'a great many' and 'a number of' take a plural noun with a plural verb, while 'the number of' takes a plural noun but a singular verb. 'More than two' takes a plural noun and plural verb.


**Examples**

- ✗ Many a man (A) were taken ill (B) after consuming illicit liquor. (C) No error (D)
  - 'Many a man' is singular, so the verb should be 'was taken ill'.
- ✗ We are (a) fortunate in being able (b) to visit Puri because there is many who cannot go there, (c) No error (d)
  - 'Many' is plural, so 'there is many' should be 'there are many'.
- ✗ A great many student (a) have been declared (b) successful (c) no error (d)
  - 'A great many' takes a plural noun: 'A great many students'.


## Rule 16: Use of Double Comparative & Double Superlative

**Topic:** Degrees of Comparison


A comparative degree must not be combined with another comparative marker, so 'more better', 'more heavier', 'more warmer' and 'beautifuler' are wrong. Similarly a superlative must not be doubled, as in 'most smartest'. Use either 'more + adjective' or the '-er/-est' form, never both.


**Examples**

- ✗ It is more better 1) if one of the parents 2) stays at home to look after the children. 3) No error 4)
  - 'Better' is already comparative; 'more better' is a double comparative.
- ✗ I am more lonelier (a) here than (b) I was in the USA. (c) No error (d)
  - Use 'lonelier' or 'more lonely', not 'more lonelier'.
- ✗ He is the (a) most smartest person (b) that I have ever met. (c) No error (d)
  - 'Smartest' is already superlative; 'most smartest' is a double superlative.


## Rule 17: Errors Related to Active & Passive Voice

**Topic:** Voice


In the passive voice the structure must be a form of 'be' plus the past participle (V3), not the -ing form or the bare base form. Intransitive verbs cannot be made passive, so forms like 'are gone', 'were providing' (for 'were provided') and 'has been picked' (for 'has picked') are errors. Choose active or passive according to whether the subject does or receives the action.


**Examples**

- ✗ The construction of the new business school is led to a sudden rise of population in our area.
- ✗ What would happen if human beings were governing by robots?
- ✗ My friends are gone on a trip to Goa today.


## Rule 18: Verb Agreement with Additive Connectives (With, Along with, As well as)

**Topic:** Subject-Verb Agreement


When two subjects are joined by with, together with, along with, besides, except, including, as well as, unlike, accompanied by, or in addition to, the verb agrees with the first subject only. The words following such connectives are parenthetical and do not change the number of the subject. So a singular first subject takes a singular verb even if the second noun is plural.


**Examples**

- ✗ The gentleman (1) together with his (2) wife and daughter were drowned. (3) No Error (4)
- ✗ I, along with my parents (a) were present (b) at the reception, (c) No error (d)
- ✗ Mr. Malhotra, including (a) his friends, were (b) invited to dinner, (c) No error (d)


## Rule 19: Correlative Conjunctions: Either-or / Neither-nor / Not only-but also

**Topic:** Conjunctions and Subject-Verb Agreement


Correlative conjunctions must be used in their fixed pairs: either...or, neither...nor, not only...but also. With either-or and neither-nor joining two subjects, the verb agrees with the subject nearer to it. The elements joined by the pair must also be parallel in structure.


**Examples**

- ✗ Neither (1) Rakesh nor (2) Sohan, presented their papers before the deadline. (3) No Error (4)
- ✗ The rural poor (1) can neither migrate to (2) the urban areas or afford the very high fees charged by the corporate private schools (3) No Error (4)
- ✗ Neither he nor I (1) was able to (2) finish the task within the time limit. (3) No Error (4)


## Rule 20: Hardly/Scarcely ... When; No Sooner ... Than

**Topic:** Conjunctions / Correlatives & Inversion


'Hardly' and 'Scarcely' are always followed by 'when' (never 'than'), while 'No sooner' is always followed by 'than' (never 'when'). When a sentence begins with Hardly/Scarcely/No sooner, the auxiliary verb must come before the subject (inversion), e.g. 'Hardly had he arrived...', 'No sooner did she see...'. The first clause normally takes past perfect and the second clause simple past.


**Examples**

- ✗ He'd no sooner (a) seen one continent (b) when he saw another (c) no error (d)
  - 'No sooner' takes 'than', not 'when': 'than he saw another'.
- ✗ Hardly he had arrived (a) when the house caught fire (b) and everything was reduced to ashes (c) No Error (d)
  - A sentence beginning with 'Hardly' requires inversion: 'Hardly had he arrived'.
- ✗ Hardly we had reached (a) the platform (b) when the train started, (c) no error (d)
  - Inversion is needed after the initial 'Hardly': 'Hardly had we reached'.


## Rule 21: Errors Related to Forms of Verbs

**Topic:** Verb Forms (V1/V2/V3)


Verbs must appear in the correct form for the tense and structure used; irregular verbs do not take '-ed' (put-put-put, burst-burst-burst, beat-beat-beaten, broadcast-broadcast-broadcast). Commonly confused pairs must be distinguished: rise/raise, lie/lay, fall/fell, hang (hung for objects, hanged for execution), lead/led, find/found (found = establish), flow/overflow, lose/loose and affect/effect. After 'have/has/had' and in passive structures the third form (V3) is required.


**Examples**

- ✗ Raman putted the vegetables in the frying pan and after adding some water, closed the lid.
- ✗ Though he had (a) a few doubts, (b) he didn't rise any question, (c) No error (d)
- ✗ Yesterday Himani woke up (A) and lied awake thinking about (B) the way her brother had responded. (C) No error (D)


## Rule 22: Errors based on Quite / Quiet

**Topic:** Confusing Words / Adverbs


'Quite' is an adverb meaning 'completely, fairly, to a considerable degree' and modifies adjectives, adverbs or verbs. 'Quiet' is an adjective/noun meaning silent or calm. Learners commonly write 'quiet' where the intensifier 'quite' is required, e.g. 'quite a generous woman', 'quite badly'. Also, 'quite' should not be combined with another intensifier like 'too'.


**Examples**

- ✗ She is quiet a generous woman (a) to have donated such a large amount (b) for the flood affected people (c) No error (d).
  - 'Quiet' (silent) must be replaced by the intensifier 'quite' — 'quite a generous woman'.
- ✗ I did not like his (1) comments on my paper (2) but I had no alternative as I had agreed to keep quite (3) No error (4)
  - The idiom is 'to keep quiet' (silent), not 'keep quite'.
- ✗ This question is quite too simple for me to answer.
  - 'Quite' and 'too' cannot be used together; it should be 'is quite simple' or 'is too simple'.


## Rule 23: Errors based on Late / Lately

**Topic:** Confusing Words / Adverbs


'Late' works as both adjective and adverb meaning 'after the expected time' (the train is running late, he came late). 'Lately' means 'recently' and refers to the recent past, usually with the present perfect (he has not been feeling well lately). Using 'lately' for delay, or 'late' for 'recently', is an error.


**Examples**

- ✗ The train is running lately (a) so we must not wait (b) for them, (c) No error, (d)
  - Delay is expressed by 'late', not 'lately' — 'the train is running late'.
- ✗ It's a little lately to go back to where we started from.
  - It should be 'a little late'; 'lately' means recently.
- ✗ His wife has (a) not been feeling (b) well late, (c) No error, (d)
  - With the present perfect meaning 'recently', use 'lately' — 'has not been feeling well lately'.


## Rule 24: Errors based on Bitter / Bitterly and Sweet / Sweetly

**Topic:** Adjectives vs Adverbs / Linking Verbs


Verbs of sensation such as taste, smell, look, sound and feel are linking verbs and take an adjective, not an adverb, as complement. So we say 'it tasted bitter/sweet', not 'bitterly/sweetly'. The adverb form is used only when the verb shows a real action (she smiled sweetly). Comparatives follow the same rule: 'taste sweeter', not 'more sweetly'.


**Examples**

- ✗ He did not eat the apple (a) because it tasted bitterly (b) and was slightly rotten (c) No error (d)
  - 'Taste' is a linking verb and takes an adjective: 'it tasted bitter'.
- ✗ She took (a) tea and (b) said that it tasted sweetly, (c) No error (d)
  - 'Tasted sweetly' should be 'tasted sweet'.
- ✗ Mangoes taste (a) more sweetly than (b) any other fruit of this world (c) No error, (d)
  - The comparative of the adjective is required: 'taste sweeter than'.


## Rule 25: Errors based on Fast / Fastly

**Topic:** Adjectives vs Adverbs


'Fast' serves as both adjective and adverb; there is no word 'fastly' in English. Hence 'he ran so fast', 'running very fast' are correct. Any occurrence of 'fastly' in a sentence is the error part.


**Examples**

- ✗ He ran so fastly (a) that he reached (b) the destination in just two minutes, (c) No error (d)
  - There is no word 'fastly'; the adverb form is 'fast' — 'he ran so fast'.
- ✗ She ran so fastly (1) that she was able to catch (2) the running bus (3) No error. (4)
  - 'So fastly' must be 'so fast'.
- ✗ He hurt himself (A) yesterday while running (B) very fastly. (C) No error (D)
  - 'Very fastly' should be 'very fast'.


## Rule 26: Errors based on Hard / Hardly

**Topic:** Confusing Words / Adverbs


'Hard' is both adjective and adverb meaning 'with great effort or force' (work hard, hit hard, die hard). 'Hardly' is a negative adverb meaning 'scarcely, almost not', so it reverses the sense of the sentence. There is no word 'hardly' meaning 'with effort', and 'hard-won' (not 'hardly won') is the correct compound adjective.


**Examples**

- ✗ The teacher advised the students (a) to work regularly (b) and hardly if they wanted to pass the examination, (c) no error (d)
  - 'Hardly' means scarcely; the intended sense is effort, so 'hard' is correct.
- ✗ Old habits (a) die (b) hardly, (c) No error (d)
  - The correct idiom is 'Old habits die hard'.
- ✗ He studied (a) so hardly (b) he was sure of passing. (c) No error (d)
  - 'So hardly' should be 'so hard'.


## Rule 27: Errors based on Farther / Further

**Topic:** Confusing Words / Comparatives


'Farther' refers to physical or measurable distance (Mumbai is farther from Delhi than Patna). 'Further' is used for additional or extra things that are not physical distance — further information, further punishment, further volumes, further investigation. Substituting 'farther' for abstract addition is the common error.


**Examples**

- ✗ If he wants farther information, send him to me.
  - Additional information requires 'further', not 'farther'.
- ✗ Mumbai is (a) further from (b) Delhi than Patna, (c) No error (d)
  - Physical distance takes 'farther' — 'farther from Delhi'.
- ✗ You will not be given (a) farther punishment, (b) so be happy now. (c) No error, (d)
  - 'Farther punishment' should be 'further punishment' (additional).


## Rule 28: Errors based on Too Much / Much Too

**Topic:** Intensifiers / Adverbs


'Too much' is used with uncountable nouns or after verbs (too much luggage, he talks too much). 'Much too' is an intensifier used before adjectives and adverbs (much too heavy, much too young, much too windy). Writing 'too much' before an adjective, or 'much too' before a noun, is the error.


**Examples**

- ✗ I advised my brother to engage two coolies instead of one (a) because the luggage was too much heavy (b) for a single coolie to handle, (c) No error (d)
  - Before the adjective 'heavy', use 'much too' — 'much too heavy'.
- ✗ His failure (a) is too much (b) painful for his family, (c) No Error (d)
  - 'Too much painful' should be 'much too painful'.
- ✗ She is too much (a) young to brave (b) this world. (c) No error, (d)
  - Before the adjective 'young', use 'much too' — 'She is much too young'.


## Rule 29: Errors based on Coward/Cowardly, Miser/Miserly, Scholar/Scholarly

**Topic:** Nouns vs Adjectives


Coward, miser and scholar are nouns, while cowardly, miserly and scholarly are the corresponding adjectives. So a person is 'a coward' or behaves 'in a cowardly manner'; 'a coward person' is wrong and should be 'a cowardly person'. Note also that 'cowardly' is an adjective, not an adverb, so 'he behaved cowardly' is incorrect.


**Examples**

- ✗ He behaved (a) cowardly (b) before the principal (c) No error (d)
  - 'Cowardly' is an adjective, not an adverb; it should be 'behaved in a cowardly manner'.
- ✗ People blamed him (1) for being (2) a coward person. (3) No Error (4).
  - 'Coward' is a noun; the adjective 'cowardly' is needed — 'a cowardly person'.


## Rule 30: Subject-Verb Agreement with Main Subject

**Topic:** Subject-Verb Agreement


The verb must agree with the main (head) subject of the sentence, not with any qualifying phrase that follows it. Phrases beginning with 'of', 'with', 'along with', 'containing', 'caused by', etc. are qualifiers and do not change the number of the subject. So 'The crew of sailors was...', 'The box containing the apples was...'. Also, gerunds and abstract nouns as subjects take singular verbs.


**Examples**

- ✗ One-to-one interaction with parents are known to be important.
- ✗ In humans, consumption of alcohol, stimulate brain cells to release mood-altering dopamine.
- ✗ Lack of required vitamins and minerals lead to several complications in the human body.


## Rule 31: So / Very / Too / Enough / As-As / So-As

**Topic:** Degrees of Comparison and Adverbs of Degree


'Very', 'so', 'too' and 'enough' are used with the positive degree, not the comparative: 'very strong', not 'very stronger'. 'Enough' follows the adjective it modifies ('wise enough'), while 'so' is followed by 'that' and 'as...as' is used for positive comparison ('as respectful as'). 'Too' means excessively and also takes the positive degree ('too weak').


**Examples**

- ✗ Vikram is very stronger than I am.
- ✗ He is faster enough to defeat the current record holder.
- ✗ Can you not knock so louder in the morning?


## Rule 32: Use of Superfluous Expressions

**Topic:** Redundancy / Superfluous Words


Avoid words that repeat meaning already carried by another word in the sentence. Common redundancies include 'discussed about', 'returned back', 'just exactly', 'violate against', 'rest of remaining', 'precedes before', 'approach near', 'supposing if', 'sufficient enough', 'repeat again', 'forbade not to', 'came across with' and 'illustrate about'. Delete the superfluous word to correct the sentence.


**Examples**

- ✗ Sakshi discussed about (1) the problem with (2) her manager very briefly. (3) No Error (4)
- ✗ If there had not been (1) such a long queue (2) I could have returned back by 11 am. (3) No Error (4)
- ✗ This happened (a) just exactly (b) five years ago (c) No error (d)


## Rule 33: Use of Conditional Sentences

**Topic:** Conditional Sentences / If-clauses


In conditional sentences the tense of the if-clause controls the tense of the main clause. In the first conditional, 'if + present simple' is followed by 'will + V1' (never 'if ... will'). In the second conditional, 'if + past simple' is followed by 'would + V1', and in the third conditional, 'if + had + V3' is followed by 'would have + V3' (never 'if ... would have'). Similarly, 'as if', 'unless', 'wish' and time clauses take the appropriate past/past perfect or present form rather than 'will/would'.


**Examples**

- ✗ If I had the opportunity A) to participate, I won B) the painting competition. C) No error D)
  - Second conditional: 'if + past simple' needs 'would + V1'. Correct: 'I would win the painting competition'.
- ✗ If you work hard, you secure good marks.
  - First conditional: the main clause takes 'will + V1'. Correct: 'you will secure good marks'.
- ✗ If my father (a) will approve (b) I will go to Bombay (c) No error (d)
  - 'Will' is not used in the if-clause. Correct: 'If my father approves'.


## Rule 34: Nouns Joined by a Preposition Stay Singular

**Topic:** Nouns / Prepositions


When two identical nouns are joined by a preposition (after, to, upon, on), both nouns must remain singular. Correct forms are 'page after page', 'town after town', 'door to door', 'pile upon pile'. Using plural nouns such as 'pages after pages' or 'doors to doors' is an error.


**Examples**

- ✗ My sister (1) has read (2) pages after pages of the Bible. (3) No error (4)
  - Two nouns joined by a preposition remain singular: 'page after page of the Bible'.
- ✗ Towns after towns were (1) conquered (2) by him but he found no peace. (3) No error (4)
  - Correct form is 'Town after town was conquered' — both nouns singular.
- ✗ Women after women (a) spoke against the (b) practice of dowry, (c) No error (d)
  - Should be 'Woman after woman spoke against...'.


## Rule 35: Between vs Among

**Topic:** Prepositions


'Between' is used for two persons or things, while 'among' (or 'amongst') is used for more than two. 'Between' is also used with 'and' (between you and me), never 'among ... and'. With reciprocal pronouns, use 'between ourselves' for two and 'among yourselves/themselves' for more than two.


**Examples**

- ✗ There is a barrier among the egghead and (1) the hoipolloi and it would be lazy (2) idealism to ignore it. (3) No error (4)
  - Two groups are compared, so 'between the egghead and the hoipolloi' is required.
- ✗ The main purpose of the visit (a) is to develop a close relationship (b) among the two countries, (c) no error (d)
  - With two countries, use 'between the two countries'.
- ✗ These customs are (a) prevalent between the inhabitants of (b) the lower regions, (c) no error (d)
  - 'Inhabitants' are more than two, so 'among the inhabitants' is correct.


## Rule 36: Beside vs Besides

**Topic:** Prepositions


'Beside' means 'by the side of' or 'next to', indicating physical position. 'Besides' means 'in addition to' or 'apart from'. Note also that neither takes 'of', so 'beside of' is incorrect.


**Examples**

- ✗ The girl lay down on the bed besides her mother and went to sleep.
  - Physical position requires 'beside her mother' (by the side of).
- ✗ The river flowed besides the hillock in our village.
  - The river flowed by the side of the hillock, so 'flowed beside' is correct.
- ✗ Do you play any other sports beside of cricket and football?
  - The sense is 'in addition to', so 'besides' should be used, and never with 'of'.


## Rule 37: Despite / Comprise / Await — No Extra Preposition

**Topic:** Prepositions with Verbs


'Despite' is never followed by 'of' (use 'despite' or 'in spite of'). 'Comprise' already means 'consist of', so 'comprises of' or 'comprises from' is wrong; use 'comprises' or 'consists of'. Similarly 'await' takes a direct object, so 'awaiting for' is incorrect — use 'awaiting the results' or 'waiting for the results'.


**Examples**

- ✗ Most politicians predict (1) that the same members will (2) be elected despite of their failure to perform. (3) no error (4)
  - 'Despite' is not followed by 'of'; use 'despite their failure' or 'in spite of their failure'.
- ✗ Despite of being a close friend he did not help me in any way.
  - Correct form is 'Despite being' or 'In spite of being'.
- ✗ Science comprises of many (a) branches of (b) learning, (c) no error (d)
  - 'Comprise' already includes 'of'; write 'Science comprises many branches' or 'consists of'.


## Rule 38: Each Other vs One Another

**Topic:** Reciprocal Pronouns


'Each other' is used when referring to two persons or things, and 'one another' is used for more than two. Neither expression can be altered to forms like 'one other'.


**Examples**

- ✗ A few politicians were at enmity (1) with each other and no one was ready (2) to budge and this is what led to the division of the party. (3) No error (4)
  - 'A few politicians' are more than two, so 'with one another' is required.
- ✗ The five brothers (1) are at daggers drawn, (2) so they feel it below their dignity to talk to each other. (3) No Error (4)
  - Five brothers are more than two, so 'to talk to one another' is correct.
- ✗ He was so afraid (1) that his knees (2) knocked one another. (3) No Error (4)
  - Knees are two, so 'knocked each other' is correct.


## Rule 39: Arrangement of Pronouns

**Topic:** Pronouns


In good news the order of pronouns is 231 (You, he and I), while in confessions of guilt it is 123 (I, you and he). The possessive adjective and reflexive form must agree with the combined subject, so 'You and I have done our best', not 'my best'.


**Examples**

- ✗ He, You and I (1) shall arrange everything (2) and shall not ask them for help. (3) No error (4)
  - For good news the order is 231 — 'You, he and I'.
- ✗ John, I and Hari have finished our studies. (a) (b) (c) No error (d)
  - The first person pronoun should come last in good news: 'John, Hari and I'.
- ✗ You, he and she have done her best (a) in the interview and it is sure (b) that all of them will pass. (c) No error (d)
  - With a plural combined subject the possessive must be 'their best', not 'her best'.


## Rule 40: Until / Unless

**Topic:** Conjunctions – Until/Unless


'Until' refers to time while 'Unless' refers to a condition. Both already carry a negative sense, so a second negative (do not, doesn't, never) must not be used in the same clause. Also, the clause after 'unless/until' takes the present tense, not 'will'.


**Examples**

- ✗ Unless you do not (1) take care of your health, (2) you will continue to suffer (3). No Error (4)
  - 'Unless' is already negative, so 'do not' is redundant; write 'Unless you take care'.
- ✗ Until you do not (1) go through your books (2) you cannot get through the examination. (3) No Error (4)
  - Double negative with 'Until'; it should be 'Unless you go through your books'.
- ✗ Until you do not go to the station (1) to receive him (2) I can hardly feel at ease. (3) No Error (4)
  - Remove the extra negative: 'Unless you go to the station'.


## Rule 41: One / ones / one's

**Topic:** Pronouns – one/ones/one's


When the indefinite pronoun 'one' is the subject, the following pronouns must be 'one', 'one's' or 'oneself', not 'his', 'her' or 'their'. The possessive form is 'one's' with an apostrophe, while the plural substitute 'ones' takes no apostrophe.


**Examples**

- ✗ One should look for his (1) own faults first (2) and then for those of others (3). No Error (4)
  - With the subject 'one', the possessive must be 'one's', not 'his'.
- ✗ One should respect (a) religious beliefs of others, (b) as much as his own. (c) no error (d)
  - 'his own' should be 'one's own' to agree with 'one'.
- ✗ One should study the history (a) of his country because it alone can satisfy (b) one's natural curiosity to know what happened in the past, (c) no error (d)
  - 'his country' should be 'one's country' after the subject 'one'.


## Rule 42: Both / Between

**Topic:** Correlative conjunctions and prepositions


'Both' is paired with 'and', never with 'as well as' or 'or'. Similarly 'between' is followed by 'and' and is used for two persons or things, whereas 'among' is used for more than two. Pronouns after 'between' take the objective case (you and me, you and him).


**Examples**

- ✗ He was selected for the post (1) because of he was familiar (2) with both urban as well as rural way of life. (3) no error (4)
  - 'both' must be paired with 'and': 'both urban and rural'.
- ✗ It is not easy for an actor (a) to earn respect from both, the fans (b) as well as the critics, (c) no error (d)
  - 'both ... as well as' is wrong; use 'both the fans and the critics'.
- ✗ Between you and I, (1) I am convinced (2) that this painting by Neetu shows greater artistry than that of Reshma. (3) No Error (4)
  - 'Between' is a preposition and takes the objective case – 'Between you and me'.


## Rule 43: No conjunction 'that' before a wh-word

**Topic:** Conjunctions


The conjunction 'that' is not used before a wh-word such as how, what, when, why, whether or 'if' in reported speech. Sentences like 'He asked that why he was not selected' are wrong; the 'that' must be dropped. Use either 'that' with a statement clause or the wh-word/if alone with a question clause, never both together.


**Examples**

- ✗ He asked the same question (1) that why he was not selected (2) and everyone responded saying that he had failed. (3) No error (4)
  - 'That' must not precede the wh-word 'why'; it should be 'why he was not selected'.
- ✗ She does not understand (a) that how one can be employed as a stenographer (b) without the necessary qualification, (c) no error (d)
  - Remove 'that' before the wh-word 'how'.
- ✗ He asked her that (1) whether she knew (2) what had happened last week when she was on leave. (3) No Error (4)
  - 'That' cannot be used before 'whether'; it should be 'He asked her whether she knew...'.


## Rule 44: Its/It's, Their/There, Your/Yours

**Topic:** Pronouns and Determiners


'Its' is the possessive form while 'it's' means 'it is/has'; 'their' shows possession while 'there' refers to a place or is used in 'there is/are'. 'Your' is used before a noun, whereas 'yours' stands alone as a possessive pronoun and is never followed by a noun. Errors occur when these homophones are interchanged.


**Examples**

- ✗ Their is direct evidence that when learning (1) occurs, neuro-chemical communication between (2) neurons is facilitated and less input is required. (3) No error (4)
  - The existential structure requires 'There is direct evidence', not the possessive 'Their'.
- ✗ "I had forgotten all such things just (1) for the sake of the greater good of everyone." (2) said there leader. (3) No error (4)
  - A possessive determiner is needed before 'leader': 'said their leader'.
- ✗ Why is it that they can develop only a (1) certain aspect of there life and not realize (2) their full potential? (3) No error (4)
  - 'There life' should be 'their life', the possessive form.


## Rule 45: Errors related to Inversion

**Topic:** Inversion / Word Order


Inversion means the auxiliary verb comes before the subject. It is required in interrogative sentences and in clauses beginning with negative or restrictive adverbs such as seldom, never, little, no sooner, under no circumstances, and in 'nor' clauses. For example, 'Never have I listened...' and 'No sooner had she realised...' are correct, while 'Never I have listened' is wrong.


**Examples**

- ✗ Seldom if ever (a) nature does operate (b) in closed and separate compartments, (c) no error (d)
  - After the negative adverb 'seldom' the clause must be inverted: 'does nature operate'.
- ✗ Never I have listened to such beautiful music (a) as the piece we heard (b) on the radio last night, (c) no error (d)
  - 'Never' at the start requires inversion: 'Never have I listened'.
- ✗ No sooner she had realised (a) her blunder than she began (b) to take corrective measures, (c) no error (d)
  - 'No sooner' requires inversion: 'No sooner had she realised'.


## Rule 46: Question tags with everyone/everybody/somebody/nobody/nothing

**Topic:** Question Tags


With indefinite pronouns referring to people (everybody, somebody, nobody, anyone), the pronoun used in the question tag is 'they'. With indefinite pronouns referring to things (everything, something, nothing, anything), the tag pronoun is 'it'. Words like nobody and nothing are already negative, so the tag takes a positive verb, e.g. 'Nobody did the work, did they?'


## Rule 47: Confusion in the usage of nouns and verbs

**Topic:** Confusable Words


Some word pairs differ only in whether they are nouns or verbs. 'Practice' and 'advice' are nouns, while 'practise' and 'advise' are verbs. So one gives a piece of advice but advises a friend, and one attends a practice session but practises English daily.


**Examples**

- ✗ If you practice (a) speaking English daily, (b) you will become fluent, (c) No Error (d)
  - The verb form is 'practise'; 'practice' is the noun.
- ✗ The football team's practise (a) session was cancelled (b) due to heavy rain, (c) No Error (d)
  - Here a noun is needed, so it should be 'practice session'.
- ✗ He gave me a piece of advise (a) regarding my career, (b) which I followed sincerely, (c) No Error (d)
  - 'A piece of advice' is required; 'advise' is the verb.


## Rule 48: Errors Related to Use of Relative Pronouns 'What' / 'That'

**Topic:** Relative Pronouns


'That' is a relative pronoun that takes an antecedent (a noun or pronoun before it), whereas 'what' does not take any antecedent and itself means 'the thing which'. So after a stated noun we must use 'that', not 'what'. Using 'what' after an expressed antecedent, as in 'the food what they cooked' or 'the information what I needed', is incorrect.


**Examples**

- ✗ He gave me all the information A) what I needed B) for the project. C) No error. D)
  - 'Information' is an expressed antecedent, so the relative pronoun 'that' should be used instead of 'what'.
- ✗ This is the book A) what he was talking about B) during the meeting. C) No error. D)
  - After the antecedent 'the book', 'that' must be used in place of 'what'.
- ✗ She told us about the challenges A) what she faced B) while travelling. C) No error. D)
  - 'The challenges' is the antecedent, so 'that she faced' is correct, not 'what she faced'.


## Rule 49: Errors Related to Numeral Adjectives

**Topic:** Numeral Adjectives


Numeral adjectives like hundred, thousand, dozen, score and units of measure remain singular when preceded by a definite number (e.g., five hundred people, two dozen mangoes, a five-man committee). They take the plural form with 'of' only when they express an indefinite quantity (hundreds of people, thousands of miles). Similarly, a numeral plus a noun used as a compound adjective stays singular, as in 'five-month-old' and 'five minutes' time' requires the possessive form.


**Examples**

- ✗ My neighbour's grandson is only five month old.
  - The compound adjective of age takes the form 'five months old' (or 'five-month-old' before a noun).
- ✗ We bought two dozens mangoes from the market.
  - After a definite number, 'dozen' stays singular: 'two dozen mangoes'.
- ✗ Only five minutes time (a) was allowed for each speaker (b) during the function. (c) No error (d)
  - The possessive form is required: 'five minutes' time'.


## Rule 50: Use of Conjunctions: 'if', 'whether' and 'that'

**Topic:** Conjunctions


'If' introduces a condition or an indirect yes/no question, while 'whether' is used when a choice or alternative (often with 'or not') is implied. 'That' introduces a statement or noun clause of certainty and must never be combined with 'if'/'whether' (never 'know that if'). In reported speech, verbs like 'ask' take 'if/whether', not 'that'.


**Examples**

- ✗ I am not sure that the red car in the parking slot is yours.
  - 'Not sure' expresses doubt/alternative, so 'whether/if' is required: 'I am not sure whether the red car...'.
- ✗ The manager said, "Could you please confirm to me that you have received all the items that you had ordered?"
  - The relative clause should be in the simple past to match the sequence: 'that you ordered'.
- ✗ He was doubtful that (a) the man who had been run over (b) by some vehicles had lain there for more than a day (c) no error (d)
  - 'Doubtful' takes 'whether/if', not 'that': 'He was doubtful whether...'.


## Rule 51: Errors Related to Articles (use of 'the')

**Topic:** Articles


The definite article 'the' is used before superlative degrees, ordinals and words like former/latter, and in comparative structures of the type 'the more... the better'. Using 'a/an' or omitting the article before a superlative or ordinal ('a best form', 'first time') is an error. Note that with 'most' meaning 'the majority of', no article is used.


**Examples**

- ✗ Athens was a most luminous of all city-states of ancient Greece.
- ✗ I had walked for most of the day to Kanpur and then got on the train to Mumbai.
- ✗ This is a worst famine this country has seen in a decade.


## Rule 52: Article with two subjects forming a single unit

**Topic:** Articles


When two nouns joined by 'and' refer to the same person or form a single unit, the article is used only before the first noun and the verb is singular. If the article is repeated before both nouns, they are treated as two separate persons/things and the verb becomes plural. Example: 'The Treasurer and Secretary is present' (one person) vs 'The Treasurer and the Secretary are present' (two people).


**Examples**

- ✗ Mr. Das, my friend (1) and Principal (2) of this college, (3) have retired. (4)
  - Answer key: 2 -> 'have retired'. 'My friend and Principal' refers to one person, so the singular verb 'has retired' is required.
- ✗ On my request (a) Lalit introduced me (b) to his friend who is singer and a scientist (c) No Error (d)
  - Answer key: c. The article is wrongly placed; it should be 'who is a singer and a scientist' (or 'a singer and scientist' if one person).
- ✗ Mr. Khan has a white (1) and a black dog (2) which guard his house from robbers. (3) No error (4)
  - Answer key: 4 -> No error. Repeating the article ('a white and a black dog') correctly indicates two dogs, so the plural verb 'guard' is right.


## Rule 53: Idiomatic phrases that do not take articles

**Topic:** Articles


Certain fixed idiomatic expressions are used without any article, such as 'by mistake', 'by air', 'in trouble', 'in conclusion', 'set on fire' and 'what sort of people'. Inserting 'a', 'an' or 'the' into these phrases is an error.


**Examples**

- ✗ It was (1) by a mistake (2) that he caught his hand. (3) No Error (4)
  - Answer key: 2. The idiom takes no article: 'by mistake'.
- ✗ Mr. Gupta leads (1) a very busy life (2) so he goes every where by a scooter. (3) No error (4)
  - Answer key: 3. Means of transport takes no article in this idiomatic use: 'by scooter'.
- ✗ He does not like (1) this type of a man (2) who does nothing but find out fault with others. (3) No error (4)
  - Answer key: 2. 'This type of man' takes no article after 'of'.


## Rule 54: Errors related to the Apostrophe ('s)

**Topic:** Apostrophe / Possessive case


The apostrophe shows possession and must be placed correctly. With compound nouns the 's is added to the last word (sister-in-law's), with joint ownership only the second noun takes 's, and separate ownership requires 's on each noun. Possessive forms of 'somebody else' become 'somebody else's', and double possessives such as 'the poor's' after 'that of' are wrong.


**Examples**

- ✗ You know it well (a) that your success in life (b) depends not on my advice but on somebody's else, (c) no error (d)
  - Answer key: C. The possessive of the compound pronoun is 'somebody else's', not 'somebody's else'.
- ✗ I visited John's and Mary's house (a) twice but found (b) the couple absent, (c) no error (d)
  - Answer key: A. For joint ownership of one house, only the last noun takes the apostrophe: 'John and Mary's house'.
- ✗ The building's roof (a) needs repairing otherwise (b) it may fall down and result in many person's death, (c) no error (d)
  - Answer key: A. Lifeless things normally take the 'of' form: 'the roof of the building'.


## Rule 55: Errors related to Parallelism

**Topic:** Parallelism


Words, phrases and clauses joined by coordinating conjunctions or correlative pairs such as 'not only... but also' must be in the same grammatical form. A mismatch of tense, verb form, or structure between the joined elements is an error, as is mixing degrees of comparison in a single parallel series.


**Examples**

- ✗ The followers of Hindu (a) crafted a gigantic temple in which (b) they could go inside and worshipped in the presence of God. (c) No error (d)
  - Answer key: C. Parallelism requires 'could go inside and worship', not 'worshipped'.
- ✗ The minister praised the (1) record voter turnout, and calling it (2) a victory for democracy irrespective of the outcome. (3) No Error (4)
  - Answer key: 2. Parallelism requires 'praised ... and called it', not 'and calling it'.
- ✗ He went up to her (a) and asks her why she had (b) insulted him. (c) No Error (d)
  - Answer key: B. The parallel verbs must share the same tense: 'went ... and asked'.


## Rule 56: Errors related to Causative Verbs

**Topic:** Causative Verbs


The causative verbs have, get, make and let follow fixed patterns. In the active voice, have/make/let take the bare infinitive while get takes 'to + V1'; in the passive voice, have/get take V3 while make/let take 'to + V1'. Examples: 'I made him do the work' / 'He was made to do the work'; 'I got him to do the work' / 'I got the work done by him'.


**Examples**

- ✗ She makes (a) her brother (b) to do his homework before dinner, (c) No Error, (d)
  - 'Make' in the active voice takes the bare infinitive: 'makes her brother do his homework'.
- ✗ The teacher let (a) the students (b) to leave early because of the heat, (c) No Error (d)
  - 'Let' in the active voice takes the bare infinitive: 'let the students leave early'.
- ✗ I will have (a) the mechanic (b) checked the car today, (c) No Error, (d)
  - Active 'have' takes the bare infinitive with a person as object: 'have the mechanic check the car'.


## Rule 57: Errors related to Fraction / Percent

**Topic:** Fractions and Percentages


With fractions, the verb agrees with the noun that follows 'of' (One third of the birds migrate; Three fourths of the pillar has rotted). Also, the numerator decides the form of the denominator: use singular 'fourth' with 'one/a' and plural 'fourths' when the numerator is more than one (three fourths, two thirds).


**Examples**

- ✗ One third of the birds (a) migrates from the southern (b) part of the country (c) No Error (d)
  - With 'one third of + plural noun (birds)', the verb must be plural: 'migrate', not 'migrates'.
- ✗ Three fourths of the (a) pillar have (b) rotted away, (c) No Error (d)
  - The noun after 'of' is singular ('pillar'), so the verb should be singular: 'pillar has'.
- ✗ Three fourth of the (a) pillars have (b) rotted away (c) No Error (d)
  - When the numerator is more than one, the denominator takes the plural form: 'Three fourths of the'.


## Rule 58: Errors related to Adjectives — Cardinals, Ordinals, Multiplicatives

**Topic:** Adjectives (Order of Numerals)


When cardinals (one, two), ordinals (first, second) and multiplicatives (single, double) occur together, the correct order is ordinal + cardinal + multiplicative, e.g. 'the first five girls', 'the first two chapters', 'the first two single rooms'. Forms like 'the five first girls' or 'the two first chapters' are incorrect.


**Examples**

- ✗ The five first girls will get the discount on our merchandise.
  - The ordinal precedes the cardinal: 'The first five girls'.
- ✗ The two first chapters of the book (1) are very interesting, (2) the remaining ones are boring. (3) No error (4)
  - Correct order is ordinal + cardinal: 'The first two chapters of the book'.
- ✗ The two first single rooms (a) of the apartment (b) were large in size, (c) No error (d)
  - Order should be ordinal + cardinal + multiplicative: 'The first two single rooms'.


## Rule 59: Arrangement of Adjectives (OSASCOMP)

**Topic:** Order of Adjectives


When several adjectives modify one noun, they follow the OSASCOMP order: Opinion, Size, Age, Shape, Colour, Origin, Material, Purpose. So it is 'long blonde hair', 'a big wooden building', 'a small black bag', and 'a small red leather bag'.


**Examples**

- ✗ His blonde long hair adds (a) glamour to his looks and long beard (b) makes him look like a saint. (c) No error (d)
  - By OSASCOMP, size comes before colour: 'His long blonde hair'.
- ✗ The beautiful, young girl (a) jumped into the river (b) in a state of depression, (c) No error (d)
  - Opinion (beautiful) correctly precedes age (young), so the sentence has no error.
- ✗ I saw a red, small, leather (a) bag lying on (b) the table, (c) no error (d)
  - Order must be size, colour, material: 'a small, red, leather bag'.


## Rule 60: Made of / Made from

**Topic:** Prepositions with 'made'


'Made of' is used when the basic material does not change in the process — a physical change (the table is made of wood; her necklace is made of gold). 'Made from' is used when the basic material changes — a chemical change (curd is made from milk; flour is made from wheat; glass is made from melted sand).


**Examples**

- ✗ These cookies are made of A) flour, sugar, and butter. B) They taste delicious. C) No Error. D)
  - The ingredients undergo a chemical change while baking, so it should be 'made from'.
- ✗ The bottle is made from A) plastic which B) is not eco-friendly. C) No Error. D)
  - The plastic remains plastic in the bottle — a physical change — so 'made of' is correct.
- ✗ The statue is made of A) bronze, B) a durable material. C) No Error. D)
  - The bronze does not change its nature, so 'made of' is correct; the sentence has no error.
